The Ag/AgCl electrode interface

A biopotential electrode converts ionic current in tissue into electronic current in a metal lead. In the silver/silver chloride electrode the conversion takes place through a reversible redox reaction at the boundary between a silver chloride layer and the electrolyte. This page develops the structure of the interface, the direction of charge transfer for each direction of the reaction, and the half-cell potential that the interface establishes.

Structure of the electrode-electrolyte boundary

The electrode is a silver conductor coated with a thin layer of silver chloride, in contact with an electrolyte containing $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$. In the metal and the lead wire charge is carried by electrons; in the electrolyte it is carried by ions.

Electrons do not enter the electrolyte, so current crosses the boundary only through a reaction that exchanges charge between the two carriers. For Ag/AgCl that reaction is

$$\text{AgCl} + e^- \;\rightleftharpoons\; \text{Ag} + \text{Cl}^-$$
  • Ag metal: the electronic conductor, connected by the lead to the amplifier input;
  • AgCl layer: a sparingly soluble salt on the silver surface, where the reaction takes place;
  • electrolyte: tissue fluid or electrode gel. $\text{Cl}^-$ takes part in the reaction; $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$ carry part of the ionic current but do not react at the surface.

Reduction, oxidation and the direction of current

The external circuit sets the direction of the reaction. Each reaction event transfers one electron, so the current through the interface is proportional to the net reaction rate:

$$i = zF\,\frac{dn}{dt}, \qquad z = 1$$

where $F = 96\,485\,\text{C/mol}$ is the Faraday constant and $dn/dt$ is the net molar rate of reaction. Three cases arise:

  • reduction ($\rightarrow$): electrons from the wire reduce AgCl to Ag and release $\text{Cl}^-$ into the electrolyte. Conventional current flows from the electrolyte into the electrode and out along the wire towards the amplifier.
  • oxidation ($\leftarrow$): $\text{Cl}^-$ combines with Ag to form AgCl and releases an electron into the metal. Conventional current flows in along the wire and out of the electrode into the electrolyte, and $\text{Cl}^-$ drifts towards the surface.
  • equilibrium: the forward and reverse rates are equal, the net current is zero and the interface rests at its equilibrium potential.

When net current flows the electrode potential departs from equilibrium by an overpotential $\eta$ whose sign follows the current, negative for cathodic (reduction) current and positive for anodic current:

$$E = E_\text{eq} + \eta$$

The Nernst equation and electrode stability

At equilibrium the potential of the interface, measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), depends on the chloride activity in the electrolyte. With unit activity for the solids Ag and AgCl, the Nernst equation gives

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{F}\ln a_{\text{Cl}^-}, \qquad E^\circ = +0.222\,\text{V}$$

At $25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ the coefficient $(RT/F)\ln 10$ is $59.2\,\text{mV}$, so each tenfold fall in chloride activity raises $E$ by $59.2\,\text{mV}$. In body fluid, with $a_{\text{Cl}^-} \approx 0.08$, the half-cell potential is about $+0.29\,\text{V}$.

  • The potential is fixed by the chloride activity, which is stable in tissue fluid and in chloride-based electrode gels.
  • Two identical electrodes in the same electrolyte have equal half-cell potentials, which cancel in a differential measurement. Any mismatch appears as a DC offset at the amplifier input.
Ag/AgCl approximates a non-polarisable electrode: the reaction exchanges charge readily, so current crosses the interface with little change in electrode potential. A polarisable electrode, such as platinum, passes current mainly by charging the double-layer capacitance, and its potential shifts with the charge passed.
