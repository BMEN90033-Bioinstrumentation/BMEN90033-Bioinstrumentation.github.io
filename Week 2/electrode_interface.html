The Ag/AgCl electrode interface

A biopotential electrode converts ionic current in tissue into electronic current in a metal lead. In the silver/silver chloride electrode the conversion takes place through a reversible redox reaction where silver, silver chloride and the electrolyte meet. This page develops the structure of the interface at atomic scale, the relation between current and overpotential, the half-cell potential, and the equivalent circuit seen by the amplifier.

01the interface

Structure of the electrode-electrolyte boundary

The electrode is silver metal carrying a layer of silver chloride, in contact with an electrolyte containing $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$. In the metal charge is carried by electrons; in the electrolyte it is carried by ions.

Electrons do not enter the electrolyte, so current crosses the boundary only through a reaction that exchanges charge between the two carriers:

$$\text{AgCl} + e^- \;\rightleftharpoons\; \text{Ag} + \text{Cl}^-$$

The scene shows a $5.7 \times 5.7\,\text{nm}$ patch of the surface with one sphere per atom or ion.

  • Ag metal: face-centred cubic silver, the electronic conductor. Electrons reach it from the lead wire.
  • AgCl grains: a rock-salt lattice of $\text{Ag}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$. Silver chloride conducts electrons poorly, so the layer is granular and porous in practice, and the pores expose silver to the electrolyte.
  • reaction sites: the base of a grain edge, where silver, silver chloride and electrolyte meet. In reduction an electron converts a lattice $\text{Ag}^+$ to a silver atom and the paired $\text{Cl}^-$ leaves through the pore. In oxidation a $\text{Cl}^-$ arrives, a silver atom gives up an electron, and the grain grows by one formula unit.
  • electrolyte: at $150\,\text{mM}$ there are 0.09 ions of each sign per $\text{nm}^3$, so the patch holds about 16 $\text{Cl}^-$. The pale shells mark the hydrated radius. $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$ do not react at the surface.

At equilibrium both reactions proceed at the same rate and no net current flows. A net current biases the ratio. The grains shrink during reduction and grow during oxidation, because one AgCl unit occupies 2.5 times the volume of the silver atom it replaces. Sustained cathodic current therefore consumes the layer, after which the electrode behaves as bare silver.

Radii, lattice spacings and ion counts are to scale. Three simplifications apply. Water is omitted: about 5700 molecules would fill the electrolyte shown. The AgCl layer is a few lattice planes thick, where a real layer is micrometres thick. The time scale is not physical: ions diffuse about $10^9$ times faster than drawn, and the ratio of forward to reverse events is illustrative.

02charge transfer

Current, overpotential and the Butler-Volmer equation

Each reaction event transfers one electron, so the current through the interface is proportional to the net reaction rate:

$$i = zF\,\frac{dn}{dt}, \qquad z = 1$$

where $F = 96\,485\,\text{C/mol}$ and $dn/dt$ is the net molar rate of reaction. The plot follows the IUPAC sign convention for the current density $j = i/A$:

  • oxidation (anodic, $j > 0$): $\text{Cl}^-$ combines with Ag to form AgCl and releases an electron into the metal. Conventional current flows from the lead into the electrolyte.
  • reduction (cathodic, $j < 0$): electrons from the lead reduce AgCl to Ag and release $\text{Cl}^-$. Conventional current flows from the electrolyte into the lead.
  • equilibrium: the two partial currents are equal and opposite, each of magnitude $j_0$, the exchange current density.

A net current requires the electrode potential to depart from equilibrium by the charge-transfer overpotential $\eta$. The anodic partial current rises exponentially with $\eta$ and the cathodic partial current falls. For a one-electron reaction with symmetric transfer coefficients,

$$j = j_a + j_c = j_0\left[e^{F\eta/2RT} - e^{-F\eta/2RT}\right] = 2j_0 \sinh\frac{F\eta}{2RT}$$

For $|\eta| \ll RT/F = 25.7\,\text{mV}$ the relation is linear and defines the charge-transfer resistance:

$$\eta \approx R_\text{ct}\, j, \qquad R_\text{ct} = \frac{RT}{F j_0}, \qquad E = E_\text{eq} + \eta$$

with $E_\text{eq}$ given by the Nernst equation of section 03.

A large $j_0$ gives a steep curve: current flows with almost no change in potential, and the electrode is non-polarisable. A small $j_0$ gives a flat curve near the origin: the potential must shift by hundreds of millivolts before appreciable current flows, and the electrode is polarisable. Ag/AgCl in chloride solution lies near the first limit. The slider value of $j_0$ is representative and is not a measured constant. At $j_0 = 1\,\text{mA/cm}^2$, $R_\text{ct} = 25.7\,\Omega\,\text{cm}^2$ and a drive of $100\,\mu\text{A/cm}^2$ shifts the potential by $2.6\,\text{mV}$.

03half-cell potential

The Nernst equation and the offset between two electrodes

At equilibrium the potential of the interface, measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), depends on the chloride activity in the electrolyte. With unit activity for the solids Ag and AgCl, the Nernst equation gives

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{F}\ln a_{\text{Cl}^-}, \qquad E^\circ = +0.222\,\text{V}$$

At $25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ the coefficient $(RT/F)\ln 10$ is $59.2\,\text{mV}$, so each tenfold fall in chloride activity raises $E$ by $59.2\,\text{mV}$. In body fluid, with $a_{\text{Cl}^-} \approx 0.08$, the half-cell potential is about $+0.29\,\text{V}$.

A biopotential is measured between two electrodes, so the amplifier sees the difference of two half-cell potentials in series with the signal:

$$\Delta E = E_1 - E_2 = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\frac{a_2}{a_1}$$
  • Two identical electrodes in the same electrolyte have equal half-cell potentials and $\Delta E = 0$.
  • A difference in chloride activity, from gel composition, drying or sweat, leaves a DC offset that is far larger than a $1\,\text{mV}$ ECG. A factor of 1.5 in activity gives $10.4\,\text{mV}$.
  • The offset is removed by AC coupling or absorbed by amplifier headroom. ANSI/AAMI EC12 limits the offset of a disposable electrode pair to $100\,\text{mV}$, and ECG amplifier standards require operation with offsets up to $\pm 300\,\text{mV}$.
The offset is harmless while it is constant. Movement of the electrode disturbs the charge distribution at the interface and changes the half-cell potential for a short time; the change passes through the amplifier as motion artefact. The stable chloride activity of a gelled Ag/AgCl electrode keeps this change small.

04equivalent circuit

Interface impedance

For small signals the interface reduces to a circuit. Charge crosses it by two parallel paths: the reaction, represented by $R_\text{ct}$, and the capacitance $C_\text{dl}$ of the ionic double layer that charges at the surface.

The half-cell potential $E_\text{hc}$ and the resistance $R_\text{s}$ of the electrolyte or gel are in series with the pair:

$$Z(j\omega) = R_\text{s} + \frac{R_\text{ct}}{1 + j\omega R_\text{ct} C_\text{dl}}$$

For an electrode of area $A$,

$$R_\text{ct} = \frac{RT}{F j_0 A}, \qquad C_\text{dl} = c_\text{dl} A, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R_\text{ct} C_\text{dl}} = \frac{F j_0}{2\pi RT\, c_\text{dl}}$$
  • Below $f_c$ the impedance is $R_\text{s} + R_\text{ct}$; above it the capacitor bypasses $R_\text{ct}$ and the impedance falls to $R_\text{s}$.
  • The double layer is about one Debye length thick, under $1\,\text{nm}$ in body fluid, which gives $c_\text{dl}$ of 10 to $40\,\mu\text{F/cm}^2$.
  • A larger area lowers the impedance at every frequency and leaves $f_c$ unchanged.

With $j_0 = 1\,\text{mA/cm}^2$ the impedance is nearly constant across the ECG band of 0.05 to $150\,\text{Hz}$. Reducing $j_0$ to $1\,\mu\text{A/cm}^2$, a polarisable electrode, raises $R_\text{ct}$ a thousandfold and moves $f_c$ into the band, so the impedance then varies with frequency by two orders of magnitude.

The electrode impedance forms a voltage divider with the amplifier input impedance. An impedance that varies across the signal band attenuates low frequencies more than high frequencies and distorts the waveform, and a mismatch between two electrodes converts common-mode interference into a differential signal. The circuit covers the electrode and gel only. On intact skin the stratum corneum adds a larger parallel RC element in series.
