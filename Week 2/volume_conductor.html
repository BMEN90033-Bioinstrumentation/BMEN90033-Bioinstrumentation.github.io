BMEN90033 · WEEK 2 · VOLUME CONDUCTION

The volume conductor problem

Biopotentials such as the ECG and EEG are recorded at the body surface, some distance from the cells that generate them. Between source and electrode lies a volume conductor: blood, muscle, fat and skin, each with its own conductivity, bounded by air. This page develops the field of a multipole source in that medium, the effect of layered tissue on each spatial harmonic, and the consequence for the signal measured between two electrodes.

01the forward problem

A multipole source in a volume conductor

The forward problem asks what potential a known current source produces at the body surface. In an unbounded homogeneous medium of conductivity $\sigma$, a current dipole of moment $p$ at the origin produces

$$\Phi(r,\theta) = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\sigma r^2}$$

where $\theta$ is measured from the dipole axis. A cardiac or cortical source is not a pure dipole. Its potential is a sum of multipole terms of increasing order $l$, each with the angular pattern of a Legendre polynomial $P_l$:

$$\Phi(r,\theta) = \sum_{l=1}^{4} a_l\,\frac{P_l(\cos\theta)}{r^{\,l+1}}, \qquad \mathbf{J} = -\sigma\nabla\Phi$$

The dipole ($l = 1$) falls as $1/r^2$, the quadrupole ($l = 2$) as $1/r^3$ and the octupole ($l = 3$) as $1/r^4$, so higher-order terms decay faster with distance from the source. A real body is neither unbounded nor homogeneous. Air does not conduct, so no current crosses the skin:

$$\left.\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial r}\right|_{r = R} = 0$$

All source current returns inside the body. For a homogeneous sphere this alone raises the surface potential of a central dipole to three times the unbounded value.

02tissue layers

Concentric shells of different conductivity

The torso and the head are not homogeneous. A standard approximation represents them as concentric spherical shells, each with a uniform conductivity. At every internal boundary the potential and the normal current density are continuous:

$$\Phi_k = \Phi_{k+1}, \qquad \sigma_k \frac{\partial \Phi_k}{\partial r} = \sigma_{k+1} \frac{\partial \Phi_{k+1}}{\partial r} \qquad \text{at } r = R_k$$

Within shell $k$ each multipole term takes the form

$$\Phi_k = \left[A_k\, r^{\,l} + B_k\, r^{-(l+1)}\right] P_l(\cos\theta)$$

The innermost shell holds the source, so its $r^{-(l+1)}$ coefficient is that of the unbounded solution. The outermost shell ends at the skin, where the radial current is zero. These conditions fix every other coefficient (Rush and Driscoll, 1968).

  • The torso model encloses the heart and its blood in lung, muscle, fat and skin. The head model encloses the brain in cerebrospinal fluid, skull and scalp.
  • The conductivities range from $1.8\,\text{S/m}$ for cerebrospinal fluid to $0.001\,\text{S/m}$ for dry skin. Lung, fat and skull are poor conductors that lie between the source and the electrodes.
  • Switching a layer off replaces its conductivity with that of the source region. With every layer off the body is a homogeneous sphere in air.
  • Both models place the source at the centre. A cortical source lies close to the skull, so the head model describes a deep source.
The skull is about 40 times less conductive than the scalp in this model. Current from a brain source spreads laterally in the cerebrospinal fluid and the scalp, which is the main reason EEG potentials are spatially smeared.

03spatial filtering

The volume conductor as a spatial low-pass filter

Relative to the unbounded homogeneous medium, the body multiplies each multipole term at the surface by a transfer coefficient $T_l$. For a single boundary between the source medium $\sigma_\text{in}$ and an outer medium $\sigma_\text{out}$,

$$T_l = \frac{(2l+1)\,\sigma_\text{in}}{l\,\sigma_\text{in} + (l+1)\,\sigma_\text{out}}$$

For a homogeneous body in air, $\sigma_\text{out} = 0$ and $T_l = (2l+1)/l$: 3 for the dipole, 2.5 for the quadrupole, falling towards 2. The amplitude of each term at the surface is then

$$|\Phi_l(R)| \propto \frac{a_l\, T_l}{R^{\,l+1}}$$

Two effects act together. The geometric factor $1/R^{\,l+1}$ already favours the dipole, and $T_l$ falls as $l$ rises, more steeply when poorly conducting shells are present. Higher multipole orders carry finer angular detail, so the volume conductor acts as a spatial low-pass filter.

The plot normalises both series to their own dipole term, on a logarithmic axis. The vertical gap between the two points of an order is the additional, order-dependent attenuation caused by the body. The overall gain $T_1$ is reported separately; it can exceed 1 because the air boundary confines the current to the body.

Surface electrodes therefore record a smoothed image of the source. Fine spatial structure in the source distribution is attenuated before it reaches the skin, which is why a single equivalent dipole describes much of the surface ECG. The head model, with its skull, filters more strongly than the torso model.

04the recorded signal

Quasi-static conduction: scaling without temporal filtering

Below about $1\,\text{kHz}$ tissue behaves as a resistive medium: capacitive currents and propagation delays are negligible. The potential then satisfies Poisson's equation at each instant,

$$\nabla \cdot \left(\sigma \nabla \Phi\right) = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}_s$$

where $\mathbf{J}_s$ is the impressed source current density. The surface potential follows the source without delay and without frequency-dependent filtering (Plonsey and Heppner, 1967). The tissue changes only the spatial weighting of the source.

  • pure dipole (source extent 0): the potential at every point of the surface is scaled by the same factor $T_1$, so the body trace is an exact scaled copy of the reference trace.
  • multipole source: each order is scaled by a different $T_l$, so the ratio of the two traces changes as the source direction changes and the waveforms differ in shape.
  • ECG: the cardiac dipole changes magnitude and direction over a $0.8\,\text{s}$ cycle ($75\,\text{bpm}$), giving P, QRS and T deflections.
  • EEG: a dipole on a fixed axis oscillates with components from $2.5$ to $35\,\text{Hz}$, dominated by the alpha band near $10\,\text{Hz}$.
Selecting ECG or EEG also selects the torso or the head model. The reference trace is the lead in an unbounded homogeneous medium; the body trace is the lead on the skin of the layered body. The display scale multiplies only the plotted body trace, so that the two can be compared in shape; the ratio readout, an RMS ratio over the visible window, is unaffected by it. The lead is sampled at $1\,\text{kHz}$, well above the highest source component.
