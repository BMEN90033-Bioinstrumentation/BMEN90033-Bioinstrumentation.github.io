The action potential as a travelling dipole

An action potential propagating through excitable tissue separates depolarised membrane from resting membrane. Each boundary between the two drives a loop of current that closes through the surrounding volume conductor, and at a distance the loop acts as a current dipole. This page follows the depolarisation and repolarisation fronts along a tissue slab, the extracellular field they produce, and the signal recorded between two surface electrodes.

01propagating fronts

Depolarisation and repolarisation fronts

A stimulus at the left end of the slab starts a depolarisation front that travels in the $+x$ direction. A repolarisation front follows a fixed distance behind it, so at any instant the tissue consists of resting tissue ahead, a depolarised plateau, and repolarised tissue behind.

Intracellular axial current flows only where the membrane potential changes along the fibre, that is, near the two fronts. At the depolarisation front it flows forward, from the depolarised region into the resting tissue ahead. At the repolarisation front it flows backward, from the depolarised plateau into the repolarised tissue. The uniformly depolarised plateau carries no axial current.

Each current loop closes through the extracellular space, so each front acts as a current dipole:

  • the depolarisation dipole $\mathbf{p}_d$ points in the direction of propagation, with the positive pole facing resting tissue;
  • the repolarisation dipole $\mathbf{p}_r$ points against the direction of propagation, so its poles are reversed;
  • both fronts travel at the same speed and stay $26$ units apart, 65 % of the slab length.
The field arrows are coloured by field magnitude on the viridis scale and are omitted from a thin strip along the slab axis, which represents the intracellular space. Electrodes A and B sit on the upper surface, 20 units apart.

02the current dipole

Potential and field of a current dipole

At distances large compared with its extent, each front is represented by a point current dipole $\mathbf{p}$ at $\mathbf{r}_0$ in a homogeneous medium of conductivity $\sigma$. With $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0$, $R = |\mathbf{R}|$ and $\hat{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{R}/R$, the potential and field are

$$\phi(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\,\frac{\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{R}}}{R^2} \qquad \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\,\frac{3(\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{R}})\,\hat{\mathbf{R}} - \mathbf{p}}{R^3}$$

The potential is positive on the side the dipole points towards and negative behind it, and falls with the square of distance; the field falls with the cube. The medium is linear, so the two fronts superpose:

$$\phi = \phi_d + \phi_r, \qquad \mathbf{p}_d = +\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \quad \mathbf{p}_r = -\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$

The model sets $4\pi\sigma = 1$, gives both dipoles unit strength, and reports potentials in arbitrary units. A front contributes only while it lies inside the slab. To avoid the singularity at the dipole, $\phi$ is set to zero for $R < 0.5$ and $\mathbf{E}$ for $R < 2$.

The plot shows $\phi$ along the upper surface of the slab, the line on which the electrodes sit, with $x$ measured from the stimulated end.

A surface point that the depolarisation front is approaching lies on the positive side of $\mathbf{p}_d$ and records a positive potential; once the front has passed, the potential is negative. The repolarisation front reverses both signs.

03bipolar recording

The differential signal between two electrodes

Electrode A records $V_A = \phi(\mathbf{r}_A)$ and electrode B records $V_B = \phi(\mathbf{r}_B)$, each relative to a distant reference. A bipolar recording measures their difference:

$$V_{AB}(t) = V_A(t) - V_B(t)$$

Electrode B lies nearer the stimulated end, so each front reaches B before A. The depolarisation front gives a positive and then a negative deflection at each electrode as it approaches and passes. The repolarisation front, with its dipole reversed, gives a negative and then a positive deflection. $V_{AB}$ combines the two electrode signals with opposite signs, so its shape depends on where both fronts lie relative to both electrodes.

A potential common to both electrodes cancels in the difference. The bipolar signal is therefore dominated by sources close to the electrodes, while distant sources, which produce similar potentials at A and B, contribute little.

The plot covers one cycle of the simulation, from the entry of the depolarisation front at $x = -2$ to the exit of the repolarisation front, and its time axis scales inversely with the speed setting. The cursor marks the present instant.

The model is quasi-static: each frame is solved as a steady-state current-flow problem. This is appropriate for biological tissue because, at physiological frequencies, conduction currents dominate displacement currents and the potential distribution follows the sources without delay.
