The Laplace transform

The Fourier transform represents a signal as a sum of sinusoids and is defined only when its integral converges. The Laplace transform adds an exponential weighting, which extends the representation to growing signals and leads to the transfer function, poles and zeros of a linear system.

01motivating example

Second-order response of a car suspension

After a car crosses a bump, its chassis oscillates before returning to equilibrium. The settling rate depends on the suspension damping.

The three designs give a well-damped ($\zeta = 0.75$), an underdamped ($\zeta = 0.12$) and an unstable ($\zeta = -0.05$) chassis displacement $z(t)$.

A conventional Fourier transform represents the frequency content of integrable signals, including decaying transients. It does not converge for a response that grows without bound.

The Laplace transform adds an exponential weighting and exposes the pole locations that determine whether each mode decays or grows.

02the complex exponential

Rotation and magnitude in the complex plane

The complex exponential $e^{j\omega t}$ traces the unit circle. Its real component is $\cos(\omega t)$ and its imaginary component is $\sin(\omega t)$.

Angular velocity gives the frequency, radius gives the amplitude, and initial angle gives the phase.

Complex exponentials are eigenfunctions of linear time-invariant systems. A sinusoidal input produces a sinusoid at the same frequency, scaled in magnitude and shifted in phase.

03the s-plane

Complex exponentials across the $s$-plane

For a general complex frequency $s = \sigma + j\omega$, the exponential $e^{st}$ has both a growth or decay rate and an oscillation frequency.

Each tile in the left panel plots the real part $\mathrm{Re}\{e^{st}\} = e^{\sigma t}\cos(\omega t)$ for the $s$ at the centre of that tile. Selecting a tile displays the full complex trajectory of $e^{st}$ in the right panel.

  • The horizontal coordinate $\sigma$ sets the growth rate: the signal decays in the left half-plane ($\sigma<0$) and grows without bound in the right half-plane ($\sigma>0$).
  • The vertical coordinate $\omega$ sets the oscillation frequency.
  • On the imaginary axis ($\sigma = 0$) the exponential is the Fourier kernel $e^{j\omega t}$, which traces a circle of constant radius.
  • On the real axis ($\omega = 0$) the exponential is $e^{\sigma t}$, which does not rotate.

04Fourier transform geometry

The Fourier integrand in the complex plane

The Fourier integrand combines the signal $g(t)$ with the complex exponential $e^{-j2\pi f t}$.

  1. The exponential $e^{-j2\pi f t}$ traces the unit circle with angle $-2\pi f t$.
  2. Multiplication by $g(t)$ scales the radius at each time.
  3. As time advances, the scaled point traces $g(t)e^{-j2\pi f t}$ in the complex plane.

05geometric interpretation

The Fourier transform as the centroid of the wound curve

Multiplying a signal $g(t)$ by $e^{-2\pi j f t}$ winds it around the origin of the complex plane at frequency $f$.

Consider the centroid of the resulting curve. For most values of $f$, the curve loops around the origin with approximate symmetry, so positive and negative contributions cancel and the centroid remains close to zero. If $f$ coincides with a frequency component of $g(t)$, this symmetry is broken: contributions accumulate on one side of the plane and the centroid is displaced.

This displacement is, up to normalisation, the Fourier transform itself. The integral $\int g(t)\, e^{-2\pi j f t}\, dt$ is the (unnormalised) centroid of the wound curve, and peaks in $|F(f)|$ correspond to winding frequencies at which the displacement is maximal.

06convergence

Divergence of the Fourier integral for growing signals

Consider the signal $e^{0.3 t}\cos(\omega_0 t)$. The wound curve spirals outward without bound, its centroid fails to converge, and the defining integral diverges.

The ordinary Fourier integral is defined for absolutely integrable signals. Extensions also cover square-integrable signals and distributions, but a growing unstable response still lacks an ordinary Fourier transform.

Multiplying the signal by a real exponential can restore convergence.

07Laplace transform

Generalisation of the Fourier transform to a complex frequency

The Fourier kernel $e^{-j\omega t}$ is replaced by $e^{-st}$, where $s = \sigma + j\omega \in \mathbb{C}$. The kernel factorises as $e^{-st} = e^{-\sigma t}\,e^{-j\omega t}$.

The second factor is the Fourier kernel; the first is a real exponential weighting that attenuates the signal for $\sigma > 0$ and amplifies it for $\sigma < 0$. Setting $\sigma = 0$ recovers the Fourier transform exactly.

Applied to the divergent signal of the previous section, the transform converges whenever $\sigma$ exceeds the growth rate of the signal. The set of $s \in \mathbb{C}$ for which the integral converges is termed the region of convergence (ROC), and is an intrinsic part of the specification of the transform.

08transfer functions

Poles and zeros of the transfer function

For a linear time-invariant system with input $x(t)$ and output $y(t)$, the ratio of their Laplace transforms defines the transfer function, $H(s) = Y(s)/X(s)$.

For the systems considered here, $H(s)$ is a rational function of $s$, and therefore admits the factored form

$$H(s) \;=\; k \, \frac{(s - z_1)(s - z_2)\cdots}{(s - p_1)(s - p_2)\cdots}.$$

The roots of the numerator are the zeros $\{z_i\}$; the roots of the denominator are the poles $\{p_i\}$. Together with the scalar gain $k$, these two sets of complex numbers determine $H(s)$ completely.

At $s = z_i$ the numerator vanishes, so $H(z_i) = 0$ and the system annihilates that mode. At $s = p_i$ the denominator vanishes, so $|H(p_i)| \to \infty$ and the response is unbounded. The frequency response, resonant behaviour, and stability of the system are consequently all determined by the location of its poles and zeros in the $s$-plane.

09Fourier and Laplace

The Fourier transform as a restriction of the Laplace transform

Varying both $\sigma$ and $\omega$ simultaneously, the magnitude $|F(s)|$ defines a surface over the complex plane. Poles appear as singularities at which the surface diverges; zeros appear as points at which it vanishes.

The Fourier transform is the restriction of this surface to the imaginary axis $\sigma = 0$. This slice represents the steady-state response of the system to pure sinusoidal inputs $e^{j\omega t}$: the magnitude of $F(j\omega)$ gives the gain and its argument gives the phase at frequency $\omega$. Evaluating $F(s)$ on any other vertical line $\sigma = \sigma_0$ yields a parallel cross-section, which represents the response to exponentially modulated sinusoids $e^{\sigma_0 t}\,e^{j\omega t}$, that is, signals whose envelope decays for $\sigma_0 < 0$ or grows for $\sigma_0 > 0$.

This viewpoint also clarifies the role of zeros. A zero located on the imaginary axis produces an exact null in the Fourier response at the corresponding frequency, since the surface meets the plane $|F(s)| = 0$ at that point.

Drag the surface to rotate it and adjust $\sigma$ to translate the slice. Poles and zeros can be dragged in the pole-zero map.

10stability and pole location

Pole location and the impulse response

For an LTI system, $H(s)$ is the Laplace transform of the impulse response $h(t)$. The location of each pole $p_i = \sigma_i + j\omega_i$ in the complex plane determines the contribution of the corresponding mode $e^{p_i t}$ to $h(t)$.

  • Poles with $\sigma_i < 0$ (left half-plane): the mode decays exponentially, and the system is asymptotically stable.
  • Poles with $\sigma_i = 0$ (on the imaginary axis): the mode is a sinusoid of constant amplitude; the system is marginally stable.
  • Poles with $\sigma_i > 0$ (right half-plane): the mode grows without bound, and the system is unstable.
  • For a complex-conjugate pole pair, proximity to the $j\omega$ axis corresponds to a small damping ratio and a lightly damped, oscillatory response.

Dragging the orange pole in the $s$-plane moves the conjugate pair and updates the impulse response $h(t)$.
