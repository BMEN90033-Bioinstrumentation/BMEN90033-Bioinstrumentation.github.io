Introduction to transfer functions

A transfer function represents the input-output relationship of a linear time-invariant system. A damped pendulum provides a canonical second-order example.

linear systems Laplace domain poles and zeros

01input-output description

Input, system and output

Each system encountered in bioinstrumentation maps an input signal to an output signal: a transducer converts acoustic pressure to voltage, an amplifier scales a voltage, and a muscle fibre converts neural excitation to mechanical contraction.

For a linear, time-invariant system, the transfer function $H(s)$ specifies the input-output dynamics under zero initial conditions.

$$Y(s) = H(s)\,X(s)$$

The coefficients of $H(s)$ contain the system parameters. Once $H(s)$ is known, the transformed output follows algebraically from $Y(s)=H(s)X(s)$.

The complex variable $s = \sigma + j\omega$ represents a generalised frequency. The steady-state sinusoidal response at a real frequency $\omega$ is recovered by the substitution $s = j\omega$, from which the magnitude (gain) and phase (delay) of the output follow directly.

02the model

The swing as a damped pendulum

A playground swing is modelled as a pendulum of rope length $L$ and bob mass $m$, subject to linear viscous damping and a gravitational restoring torque, and driven by a periodic external force. The objective is to determine the steady-state amplitude as a function of the forcing frequency.

Derivation in five steps

Step 1: coordinate definition

Let $\theta(t)$ denote the angular displacement from vertical, taken positive in the direction of forcing. The corresponding arc-length kinematics are

$$s = L\theta, \qquad \dot{s} = L\dot{\theta}, \qquad \ddot{s} = L\ddot{\theta}.$$

Step 2: torque balance about the pivot

Newton's second law for rotation gives $I\ddot{\theta} = \sum \tau$, with $I = mL^2$ the moment of inertia of a point mass at radius $L$. Three torques act about the pivot:

  • Gravity acts vertically with magnitude $mg$; its moment arm about the pivot is $L\sin\theta$, yielding a restoring torque $\tau_g = -mgL\sin\theta$.
  • Viscous dissipation (aerodynamic drag and pivot friction) is, to leading order, linear in angular velocity: $\tau_d = -b\,\dot{\theta}$.
  • External forcing $F(t)$ applied tangentially at radius $L$ contributes $\tau_p = F(t)\,L$.

Combining these contributions:

$$mL^2\,\ddot{\theta} = -mgL\sin\theta - b\,\dot{\theta} + F(t)\,L.$$

Step 3: small-angle approximation

For modest displacements the Taylor expansion $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/6 + \ldots$ is truncated at first order, an approximation with an error of about $2\%$ at $\theta = 20^\circ$. The governing equation thereby becomes linear:

$$mL^2\,\ddot{\theta} + b\,\dot{\theta} + mgL\,\theta = F(t)\,L.$$

Each term has a direct physical interpretation:

$$\underbrace{mL^2\,\ddot{\theta}}_{\text{inertia}} + \underbrace{b\,\dot{\theta}}_{\text{damping}} + \underbrace{mgL\,\theta}_{\text{gravity restoring}} = \underbrace{F(t)\,L}_{\text{forcing}}.$$

Step 4: normalisation to canonical form

Dividing by $mL^2$ so that the coefficient of $\ddot{\theta}$ is unity:

$$\ddot{\theta} + \frac{b}{mL^2}\,\dot{\theta} + \frac{g}{L}\,\theta = \frac{F(t)}{mL}.$$

Comparison with the canonical second-order form $\ddot{\theta} + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot{\theta} + \omega_0^2\,\theta = u(t)$ identifies:

$$\omega_0^2 = \frac{g}{L} \;\Rightarrow\; \omega_0 = \sqrt{\tfrac{g}{L}}, \qquad 2\zeta\omega_0 = \frac{b}{mL^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \zeta = \frac{b}{2mL^2\omega_0} = \frac{b}{2mL\sqrt{gL}}.$$

With forcing $u(t) = F(t)/(mL)$, the equation of motion takes the canonical form:

$$\ddot{\theta} + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot{\theta} + \omega_0^2\,\theta = u(t).$$
  • Natural frequency: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$ is the undamped oscillation frequency. For $L = 2.45\text{ m}$, $\omega_0 \approx 2.0\text{ rad/s}$ (period $\approx 3.1\text{ s}$).
  • Damping ratio: $\zeta$ is a dimensionless measure of dissipation. Typical playground swings exhibit $\zeta \approx 0.02$–$0.05$, corresponding to light damping.

Step 5: Laplace transform

The Laplace transform maps differentiation to multiplication by $s$. Under zero initial conditions, $\theta(0) = \dot{\theta}(0) = 0$, each term transforms as:

$$\mathcal{L}\{\ddot{\theta}\} = s^2\,\Theta(s), \qquad \mathcal{L}\{\dot{\theta}\} = s\,\Theta(s), \qquad \mathcal{L}\{\theta\} = \Theta(s), \qquad \mathcal{L}\{u(t)\} = U(s).$$

Substitution into the canonical equation gives:

$$s^2\,\Theta(s) + 2\zeta\omega_0\, s\,\Theta(s) + \omega_0^2\,\Theta(s) = U(s).$$

Factoring $\Theta(s)$:

$$(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0\, s + \omega_0^2)\,\Theta(s) = U(s).$$

The ratio of output to input defines the transfer function, stated below.

The ratio $\Theta(s)/U(s)$ is the transfer function of the pendulum:

$$H(s) = \frac{\Theta(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{1}{s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0\, s + \omega_0^2}.$$

This is the canonical second-order low-pass response. RLC circuits, seismometers and analogue ECG filter stages share this structure; only the physical interpretation of $\omega_0$ and $\zeta$ differs.

03resonance

Resonance under periodic forcing

In practice the forcing is impulsive and not continuously sinusoidal. A brief push delivered once per cycle forms an impulse train at rate $\omega$, whose fundamental Fourier component lies at $\omega$. The transfer function determines the steady-state amplitude of this component:

$$|H(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_0\omega)^2}}$$

Maximum amplitude is attained at $\omega = \omega_0$, where the forcing remains in phase with the motion; at other frequencies the phase relationship is incoherent and amplification is suppressed. This phase dependence is captured by $|H(j\omega)|$.

For $\omega \ll \omega_0$ or $\omega \gg \omega_0$ the response is small. At $\omega = \omega_0$ the amplitude reaches $1/(2\zeta\omega_0^2)$, which is $1/(2\zeta)$ times the static ($\omega \to 0$) response: approximately ten times for the parameters shown.

The peak of the Bode magnitude defines the quality factor $Q \approx 1/(2\zeta)$. Lightly damped systems exhibit tall, narrow peaks; heavily damped systems exhibit broad, flat responses. Increasing $\zeta$ progressively flattens the peak.

04the s-plane

Pole-zero representation

The transfer function carries information beyond its values on the real-frequency axis. With $s$ treated as a complex variable, $H(s)$ diverges at some points and vanishes at others. These points, termed poles and zeros respectively, together fully characterise the system.

Equating the denominator of $H(s)$ to zero and solving the resulting quadratic:

$$s = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm j\,\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$$

The pendulum possesses two poles and no finite zeros. Writing $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, the poles are located at $-\zeta\omega_0 \pm j\omega_d$.

Interpretation of the s-plane

  • Real part $\sigma = -\zeta\omega_0$ governs the decay rate; greater magnitude corresponds to faster decay. The system is stable when both poles lie in the left half-plane.
  • Imaginary part $\pm\omega_d$ is the damped oscillation frequency; for light damping $\omega_d \approx \omega_0$.
  • Distance from the origin equals $\omega_0$, independent of damping. Varying $L$ changes $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$ and moves the poles radially at constant angle; varying $\zeta$ moves them along an arc of radius $\omega_0$.
  • Angular displacement $\phi$ from the negative real axis satisfies $\cos\phi = \zeta$.

As the damping tends to zero the poles approach the imaginary axis and the impulse response persists indefinitely. Increasing $\zeta$ moves the poles inward along the arc of radius $\omega_0$ and accelerates the decay. At $\zeta = 1$ the poles coalesce on the real axis and oscillation is superseded by pure exponential decay.

Pole positions encode the principal qualitative features of the system: stability (left half-plane), oscillation frequency (imaginary part), decay rate (real part), and resonance sharpness (proximity to the imaginary axis).

05origin of zeros

A double pendulum with an intermediate mass

The transfer function of the simple pendulum contains poles but no finite zeros. A transmission zero can arise when a second dynamical mode opposes the first through the same input channel.

The minimal modification introduces an intermediate mass $m_1$ at a joint along the rope, with the bob $m_2$ suspended below. Horizontal forcing is applied to $m_2$. The resulting system is a double pendulum with upper segment length $L_1$ and lower segment length $L_2$. The controls set the mass ratio $\mu = m_1/m_2$ and the length fraction $\alpha = L_1/L$, where $L = L_1 + L_2$.

Equations of motion

Let $\theta_1,\theta_2$ denote the two segment angles. In the small-angle limit the horizontal position of the bob is $x_2 = L_1\theta_1 + L_2\theta_2$. A Lagrangian formulation with gravity as the restoring force yields a coupled $2\times 2$ system. The off-diagonal inertia term $m_2 L_1 L_2$ couples the two angular coordinates. Together with the intermediate mass, this coupling generates a transmission zero.

Frequency of the transmission zero

$$\omega_z^2 = \frac{g\bigl[L_1 m_2 + L_2(m_1+m_2)\bigr]}{m_1 L_1 L_2}$$

At $\omega = \omega_z$ the intermediate mass oscillates with the amplitude and phase required to cancel the forcing transmitted to the bob. In the time trace, green denotes the bob displacement $x_2$ and violet denotes the intermediate-mass displacement $x_1$.

06frequency response

Frequency response from pole and zero distances

A real forcing frequency $\omega$ corresponds to the single point $s = j\omega$ on the positive imaginary axis. As $\omega$ increases, this point moves up the axis, and at each position the magnitude of the transfer function is

$$|H(j\omega)| = \frac{\prod_i |j\omega - z_i|}{\prod_k |j\omega - p_k|}$$

This is the product of distances from the marker to each zero, divided by the product of distances to each pole. Proximity to a pole produces a resonance peak; coincidence with a zero produces a transmission notch.

As $\mu \to 0$ the intermediate mass vanishes, the zero recedes to infinity, and the transfer function reduces to that of the simple pendulum. Increasing $\mu$ deepens the notch. Changing $\alpha$ shifts $\omega_z$: the notch frequency is minimised at $\alpha^* = \sqrt{1+\mu}/(1+\sqrt{1+\mu})$ (about $0.56$ for $\mu = 0.6$) and rises as the intermediate mass moves toward either end of the rope.

The 660-tonne tuned mass damper in Taipei 101 applies this mechanism. The tower is the primary mass and the suspended pendulum is the secondary mass. Its parameters are selected to attenuate wind-driven sway near the structural resonance.
