BMEN90033 · WEEK 7 · BIOINSTRUMENTATION

Removing powerline noise

The standard decoupling network for an operational amplifier places a bulk capacitor, commonly $10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$, in parallel with a small ceramic capacitor, commonly $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$, at each supply terminal. Under the ideal capacitor model the total capacitance is $10.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ and the small device contributes negligibly. In practice, omitting the small capacitor degrades the power-supply rejection of precision analogue front-ends and can cause instability. Physical capacitors have parasitic inductance and resistance, and the two components of the standard pair are effective over different frequency ranges.

01supply noise and PSRR

Coupling of supply-rail disturbances to the amplifier output

The output voltage of an operational amplifier is formed from charge drawn from its positive and negative supply rails. Because the transistors of the output stage have finite transconductance and finite output conductance, any perturbation of the rail potential is transferred to the output with a non-zero, frequency-dependent coupling coefficient.

This coupling is quantified by the power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR), defined as the ratio of a perturbation at the supply to the resulting perturbation at the output, referred to the input. Precision devices typically have a low-frequency PSRR of the order of $100\,\mathrm{dB}$. Above a break frequency of a few hundred hertz the rejection falls at approximately $20\,\mathrm{dB}$ per decade, reaching $20\,\mathrm{dB}$ in the vicinity of $1\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and becoming negligible above $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$. In the high-frequency band the amplifier therefore provides little attenuation between supply perturbations and the output signal.

The sources of such perturbations are intrinsic to the system. Switch-mode regulators inject a fundamental at the switching frequency, commonly $500\,\mathrm{kHz}$ to several megahertz, together with its harmonics. Digital logic elsewhere on the printed circuit board draws pulsed currents through the distributed inductance of the supply network, producing wideband transient voltages. The output stage of the amplifier itself demands transient current that the upstream regulator, whose control loop typically has a bandwidth of a few kilohertz, cannot supply within the relevant time scale.

A bypass capacitor provides a low-impedance path from the supply terminal to the reference potential. It limits rail-voltage disturbances and supplies transient load current locally.

02the ideal capacitor

Bypass impedance under the ideal capacitor model

The impedance of the ideal capacitor is

$$Z_C(j\omega) = \frac{1}{j\omega C}, \qquad |Z_C| = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}.$$

On logarithmic axes the magnitude response is a straight line of slope $-20\,\mathrm{dB}$ per decade, so each decade of frequency reduces the impedance by a decade. Under this model a $10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ capacitor presents $1.6\,\Omega$ at $10\,\mathrm{kHz}$, $160\,\mathrm{m}\Omega$ at $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$, and $16\,\mathrm{m}\Omega$ at $1\,\mathrm{MHz}$. A single large capacitor would then provide the lowest attainable bypass impedance at every frequency. A $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ capacitor added in parallel, whose ideal impedance exceeds that of the bulk device by two orders of magnitude at all frequencies, would provide only a marginal improvement.

If the ideal model were exact, the total capacitance would be $10.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$, the combination would present the impedance of a slightly larger single capacitor, and the smaller device would serve no purpose. Measured impedance spectra of physical capacitors do not follow this model. The ideal parallel impedance, valid only in the low-frequency limit, is

$$Z_{\text{parallel}}(j\omega) = \frac{1}{j\omega(C_1 + C_2)}.$$

03the physical capacitor

Parasitic inductance and resistance of a physical capacitor

A physical capacitor departs from the ideal model in two systematic respects. Its terminals, internal electrodes and interconnect enclose a non-zero loop area through which the charging and discharging currents flow, and this loop has a finite self-inductance. Dissipative mechanisms in the dielectric, the electrodes and the terminations contribute a non-zero series resistance. The component is therefore represented by the lumped series connection of the nominal capacitance $C$, an equivalent series inductance $\mathrm{ESL}$, and an equivalent series resistance $\mathrm{ESR}$.

Equivalent circuit of a physical capacitor

$R_p$ is the dielectric leakage resistance, typically $\mathrm{M\Omega}$ to $\mathrm{T\Omega}$. At bypass frequencies its admittance is negligible compared with $j\omega C$, so it is omitted from the expression for $Z(j\omega)$ below.

$$Z(j\omega) = \mathrm{ESR} + j\omega\,\mathrm{ESL} + \frac{1}{j\omega C}.$$

The corresponding magnitude response has a V-shaped profile on logarithmic axes. At sufficiently low frequencies the term $1/(\omega C)$ dominates and the impedance decreases at $-20\,\mathrm{dB}$ per decade, in accordance with the ideal model. At sufficiently high frequencies the term $\omega\,\mathrm{ESL}$ dominates, the impedance increases at $+20\,\mathrm{dB}$ per decade, and the component behaves as a series inductor. The transition between these two regimes occurs at the self-resonant frequency,

$$f_{\text{SRF}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\mathrm{ESL}\cdot C}},$$

at which the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel and the impedance reaches a minimum equal to the $\mathrm{ESR}$.

Scaling of self-resonance with capacitance

The dependence of $f_{\text{SRF}}$ on $C$ must account for the change in device geometry that accompanies a change in capacitance. An electrolytic or tantalum capacitor of $10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ typically occupies a comparatively large case, has an extended internal current path, and has an $\mathrm{ESL}$ of order $5$ to $10\,\mathrm{nH}$. The corresponding self-resonant frequency is

$$f_{\text{SRF,bulk}} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(5\,\mathrm{nH})(10\,\mu\mathrm{F})}} \approx 710\,\mathrm{kHz}.$$

Above this frequency the device no longer behaves capacitively, and its impedance increases with frequency. A ceramic capacitor of $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ in an 0603 or 0402 surface-mount package has a short internal current path and an $\mathrm{ESL}$ of approximately $0.5$ to $1\,\mathrm{nH}$. Its self-resonant frequency is therefore

$$f_{\text{SRF,cer}} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(1\,\mathrm{nH})(0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F})}} \approx 16\,\mathrm{MHz}.$$

Although its capacitance is two orders of magnitude smaller, the ceramic capacitor is self-resonant at a frequency more than twenty times higher than the electrolytic. This follows from the dependence of $f_{\text{SRF}}$ on the geometric mean of $\mathrm{ESL}$ and $C$: the reduction in capacitance by a factor of one hundred and the accompanying reduction in parasitic inductance by a factor of approximately five both raise $f_{\text{SRF}}$, together by a factor of $\sqrt{500} \approx 22$.

The two components are effective over different frequency ranges. The $10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ device provides the lower impedance at low and intermediate frequencies. Above its self-resonance it becomes inductive, while the $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ ceramic remains capacitive to a higher frequency.

04the parallel combination

Impedance of the $10\,\mu\mathrm{F} \parallel 0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ pair

The parallel connection of the two devices has an admittance equal to the sum of the individual admittances, from which the composite impedance follows:

$$Y_{\text{tot}}(j\omega) = Y_{\text{bulk}}(j\omega) + Y_{\text{cer}}(j\omega), \qquad Z_{\text{tot}}(j\omega) = \frac{1}{Y_{\text{tot}}(j\omega)}.$$

At any given frequency the current divides between the two branches in inverse proportion to their impedances, so the composite response is governed by the branch of lower impedance. Below the self-resonance of the bulk device the $10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ branch dominates, since its capacitive reactance is much smaller than that of the $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ branch. Above the bulk self-resonance the impedance of the $10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ device increases with frequency while that of the $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ device continues to decrease towards its own self-resonance. Above the frequency at which the two impedances are equal, the ceramic capacitor carries most of the high-frequency noise current. The composite impedance therefore follows the lower envelope of the two individual curves, giving an extended interval of low impedance that neither component provides alone.

The exception is a band around the crossover, about $2$ to $13\,\mathrm{MHz}$ for the default values. There the inductive bulk branch and the capacitive ceramic branch form a parallel resonance and $|Z_{\text{tot}}|$ rises above the lower envelope, exceeding both curves between about $5$ and $8\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and reaching about $0.6\,\Omega$ near $6.5\,\mathrm{MHz}$.

The composite does not behave as a $10.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ capacitor. The effective bypass capacitance at any frequency is set by the component that presents the lower impedance at that frequency. The additive rule holds only in the low-frequency limit, in which both components are well described by the ideal model and the contribution of the smaller capacitor is negligible. Above the impedance crossover, near $6.5\,\mathrm{MHz}$ for the default values, the bulk component contributes little bypass admittance, and the composite behaves as a $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ capacitor with $\mathrm{ESL} \approx 1\,\mathrm{nH}$.

The output stage draws transient current above the self-resonant frequency of the bulk capacitor. A nearby ceramic capacitor supplies this high-frequency current and reduces the resulting rail-voltage disturbance. The bulk capacitor restores charge at lower frequencies, where its larger capacitance provides the lower impedance.

Implications for printed circuit board layout

  • The ceramic capacitor must be placed as close as possible to the supply terminal of the amplifier. Any interconnecting trace adds loop inductance in series with the intrinsic $\mathrm{ESL}$ of the component, which lowers the self-resonant frequency and raises the bypass impedance above it.
  • The placement of the bulk capacitor is less critical, because it governs mid-frequency decoupling. Trace lengths of several centimetres are typically tolerable.
  • Connecting several ceramic capacitors of the same value in parallel reduces the total $\mathrm{ESL}$ in inverse proportion to the number of devices. The mechanism is the same as that of the bulk and ceramic pair, applied within a single decade of capacitance.
  • The effective capacitance of class-2 ceramic dielectrics, including X7R and Y5V, depends strongly on the applied DC bias and on temperature. Devices intended to provide a specified high-frequency impedance should be selected with a voltage rating of at least twice the nominal supply voltage, and the derated capacitance should be verified against the manufacturer's bias characteristic.

Capacitance value alone does not determine bypass performance. Package geometry and construction determine the current-path inductance and therefore the useful frequency range. A sufficiently large ceramic capacitor with low ESL can replace a separate bulk and ceramic pair when its bias-dependent capacitance remains adequate.

05transfer function representation

Transfer function of the bypass network

The argument of the preceding section can be confirmed formally from the transfer function of the bypass network. Treated as a linear time-invariant two-terminal element, each capacitor branch has an impedance that is a rational function of the Laplace variable $s$.

Branch impedance

The series connection of $\mathrm{ESR}_k$, $\mathrm{ESL}_k$, and $C_k$ gives, for the $k$-th branch,

$$\begin{aligned} Z_k(s) &= \mathrm{ESR}_k + s\,\mathrm{ESL}_k + \frac{1}{sC_k} = \frac{D_k(s)}{sC_k}, \\[14pt] \text{where}\quad D_k(s) &\equiv s^2\,\mathrm{ESL}_k C_k + s\,\mathrm{ESR}_k C_k + 1. \end{aligned}$$

The branch admittance $Y_k(s) = sC_k / D_k(s)$ therefore has a simple zero at the origin and a pair of poles at the roots of $D_k(s)$. Under the low-damping condition $\mathrm{ESR}_k^2 < 4\,\mathrm{ESL}_k/C_k$, these roots form a complex-conjugate pair in the left half-plane with natural frequency $\omega_{0,k} = 1/\sqrt{\mathrm{ESL}_k C_k}$, which is the self-resonant frequency of the branch.

Composite impedance

Since admittances add in parallel, the composite admittance is

$$Y_{\text{tot}}(s) = Y_1(s) + Y_2(s) = \frac{s\,\bigl[C_1 D_2(s) + C_2 D_1(s)\bigr]}{D_1(s)\,D_2(s)},$$

and the composite impedance is the rational function

$$Z_{\text{tot}}(s) = \frac{D_1(s)\,D_2(s)}{s\,\bigl[C_1 D_2(s) + C_2 D_1(s)\bigr]}.$$

The numerator is of degree four and the denominator of degree three. $Z_{\text{tot}}(s)$ is therefore an improper rational function with four zeros and three poles, whose positions in the $s$-plane are interpreted below.

Zero structure

The zeros of $Z_{\text{tot}}(s)$ are the roots of $D_1(s)$ and $D_2(s)$, and correspond to the self-resonant frequencies of the two individual branches. Each conjugate pair lies close to the imaginary axis at a distance $\alpha_k = \mathrm{ESR}_k / (2\,\mathrm{ESL}_k)$ set by the dissipation of the corresponding branch. On the imaginary axis these zeros appear as the two local minima of $|Z_{\text{tot}}(j\omega)|$ in the plot of the preceding section.

Pole structure and anti-resonance

The denominator factorises into a pole at $s = 0$, corresponding to the open-circuit behaviour of capacitors at DC, and the roots of the quadratic

$$C_1 C_2 (\mathrm{ESL}_1 + \mathrm{ESL}_2)\,s^2 + C_1 C_2 (\mathrm{ESR}_1 + \mathrm{ESR}_2)\,s + (C_1 + C_2) = 0.$$

In the underdamped regime these roots form a complex-conjugate pair with natural frequency

$$\small \omega_{\text{AR}} = \sqrt{\frac{C_1 + C_2}{C_1 C_2\,(\mathrm{ESL}_1 + \mathrm{ESL}_2)}} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{ESL}_1\,C_2}} \quad \bigl(C_1 \gg C_2,\; \mathrm{ESL}_1 \gg \mathrm{ESL}_2\bigr).$$

The approximation shows that the resonance of the composite network is governed by the equivalent series inductance of the bulk device and the capacitance of the ceramic device. Physically, this is the frequency at which the inductive reactance of the bulk capacitor above its self-resonance and the capacitive reactance of the ceramic capacitor below its self-resonance form a parallel LC tank. For the nominal parameters of the present analysis this gives $f_{\text{AR}} \approx 6.5\,\mathrm{MHz}$, between the two self-resonances.

A conjugate pole pair of small real part produces a local maximum of $|Z_{\text{tot}}(j\omega)|$ at $\omega \approx \omega_{\text{AR}}$. This anti-resonance lies between the two self-resonant minima. The combined ESR damps the parallel tank.

The transfer function analysis reproduces the two features identified in Section 4. Away from the anti-resonance the composite impedance follows the lower envelope of the two individual curves, because the zeros of $Z_{\text{tot}}$ are inherited from the individual branches. The effective capacitance of the pair at any frequency is set by the branch that presents the lower impedance. The additive rule $C_{\text{eff}} = C_1 + C_2$ is recovered only in the limit $s \to 0$, where both $D_k(s) \to 1$ and $Z_{\text{tot}}(s) \to 1/[s(C_1 + C_2)]$.

Physical capacitors are constrained by their parasitic elements. The useful bandwidth of any bypass component is bounded above by the frequency at which the equivalent series inductance replaces the capacitive reactance as the dominant contribution to the terminal impedance.
