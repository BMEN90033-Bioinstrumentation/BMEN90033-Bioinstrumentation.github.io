Modelling filters

Four canonical second-order responses built from elementary passive networks. For each topology the transfer function $H(s)$ is derived from Kirchhoff's laws, its poles and zeros are located in the $s$-plane, and the steady-state response to a composite sinusoidal input is examined. The circuit, the transfer function, the pole-zero diagram and the frequency response are four descriptions of the same filter; each section shows how they correspond.

01low-pass filter

First-order RC low-pass network

Consider a resistor $R$ in series with a capacitor $C$, driven by an input voltage $v_{\text{in}}(t)$, with the output $v_{\text{out}}(t)$ taken across the capacitor. The capacitor presents an impedance $Z_C(s) = 1/(sC)$ in the Laplace domain, and the network forms a voltage divider between $R$ and $Z_C$.

$$H(s) = \frac{V_{\text{out}}(s)}{V_{\text{in}}(s)} = \frac{1/(sC)}{R + 1/(sC)} = \frac{1}{1 + sRC}.$$

Defining the cutoff frequency $\omega_c = 1/(RC)$ gives the canonical first-order low-pass form

$$H(s) = \frac{\omega_c}{s + \omega_c}.$$

The transfer function has a single pole at $s = -\omega_c$ on the negative real axis and no finite zeros. At $\omega = 0$ the capacitor is open-circuit and $H(0) = 1$. At $\omega \to \infty$ the capacitor is a short circuit and $|H(j\omega)| \to 0$. The magnitude passes through $1/\sqrt{2}$ (the $-3$ dB point) at $\omega = \omega_c$ and rolls off asymptotically at $20$ dB per decade.

Relation between the circuit and $H(s)$

The pole location $s = -1/(RC)$ is the reciprocal of the network time constant $\tau = RC$. The same quantity that governs the exponential charging of the capacitor in the time domain determines the corner frequency in the Laplace domain. The choice of component values $R$ and $C$ therefore fixes both the transient duration and the frequency response.

cutoff frequency $\omega_c$1.00
Applicability. The RC low-pass is used as an anti-aliasing prefilter prior to analogue-to-digital conversion, as a smoothing stage after pulse-width-modulation demodulation, and as the dominant bandwidth-limiting element in instrumentation amplifiers for biopotentials such as the electrocardiogram.

02high-pass filter

First-order RC high-pass network

Interchanging the positions of the resistor and capacitor produces the dual topology: the capacitor $C$ is placed in series with the input, and the output is taken across the resistor $R$ to ground. The voltage divider now contains $Z_C(s) = 1/(sC)$ in the upper arm and $R$ in the lower arm.

$$H(s) = \frac{R}{R + 1/(sC)} = \frac{sRC}{1 + sRC}.$$

With $\omega_c = 1/(RC)$ this reduces to

$$H(s) = \frac{s}{s + \omega_c}.$$

The denominator is identical to the low-pass case, so the filter shares the same pole at $s = -\omega_c$. The numerator introduces a zero at the origin. At $\omega = 0$ the capacitor blocks the direct-current component and $|H(0)| = 0$. At $\omega \to \infty$ the capacitor acts as a short circuit and $|H(j\omega)| \to 1$. The magnitude reaches $1/\sqrt{2}$ at $\omega = \omega_c$ and approaches the asymptotic pass gain at $+20$ dB per decade.

Relation between the circuit and $H(s)$

The zero at the origin is an algebraic consequence of the series capacitor: evaluating $H(s)$ at $s = 0$ corresponds to the steady direct-current case, for which the capacitor carries no current and the output voltage across $R$ must vanish. The pole location is unchanged because the impedance sum $R + 1/(sC)$ forms the denominator of both topologies.

cutoff frequency $\omega_c$1.00
Applicability. The RC high-pass removes the slowly varying baseline component from biopotential recordings: electrode half-cell potentials, respiration-induced drift, and low-frequency motion artefact all lie below the diagnostic band and are attenuated by placement of $\omega_c$ below the signal bandwidth.

03band-pass filter

Series RLC band-pass network

Adding an inductor to the high-pass network produces a series RLC circuit: $L$, $C$, and $R$ in series between the input terminal and ground, with the output taken across the resistor. The total impedance seen by the source is $Z(s) = sL + 1/(sC) + R$, and the transfer function is the fraction of this impedance contributed by $R$.

$$H(s) = \frac{R}{sL + 1/(sC) + R} = \frac{s R/L}{s^2 + s R/L + 1/(LC)}.$$

Introducing the resonant frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and the quality factor $Q = \omega_0 L / R$ yields the second-order standard form

$$H(s) = \frac{(\omega_0/Q)\,s}{s^2 + (\omega_0/Q)\,s + \omega_0^2}.$$

The transfer function possesses a zero at the origin and a conjugate pair of poles at $s = -\omega_0/(2Q) \pm j\omega_0\sqrt{1 - 1/(4Q^2)}$. The magnitude is zero at both direct-current and infinite frequency, and reaches its maximum value of unity at $\omega = \omega_0$, where the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel and the impedance reduces to $R$ alone. The $-3$ dB bandwidth is $B = \omega_0 / Q$, and increasing $Q$ narrows the passband while leaving the centre frequency unchanged.

Relation between the circuit and $H(s)$

The quadratic denominator arises because the circuit contains two independent energy-storage elements, $L$ and $C$. The resonance condition $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ is the frequency at which the stored magnetic and electric energies exchange coherently; the damping $\omega_0/Q = R/L$ is set by the resistive loss that removes energy from this exchange. The zero at the origin again arises because the series capacitor blocks any steady component of the input.

quality factor $Q$3.00
Applicability. Band-pass filtering is used to isolate a frequency band of interest in the presence of both low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise. Typical bioinstrumentation examples include the $0.5$–$150$ Hz diagnostic band of the electrocardiogram, the alpha-rhythm band of the electroencephalogram, and the pulsatile component of the photoplethysmogram.

04notch filter

Series RLC notch (band-stop) network

Retaining the series RLC topology but relocating the output terminals so that $v_{\text{out}}$ is taken across the LC branch rather than across $R$ produces the complement of the band-pass filter. The output impedance is now $sL + 1/(sC)$, and

$$H(s) = \frac{sL + 1/(sC)}{sL + 1/(sC) + R} = \frac{s^2 + 1/(LC)}{s^2 + s R/L + 1/(LC)}.$$

With the same definitions $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and $Q = \omega_0 L / R$ this becomes

$$H(s) = \frac{s^2 + \omega_0^2}{s^2 + (\omega_0/Q)\,s + \omega_0^2}.$$

The denominator is identical to that of the band-pass filter, so the pole pair is unchanged. The numerator now carries a pair of conjugate zeros at $s = \pm j\omega_0$, located on the imaginary axis. At $\omega = \omega_0$ the factor $(j\omega_0)^2 + \omega_0^2 = 0$ and the response vanishes identically, producing an ideal transmission null. Away from the notch, $|H(j\omega)| \to 1$ at both low and high frequency.

Relation between the circuit and $H(s)$

The zero on the imaginary axis corresponds to the series resonance of $L$ and $C$: at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ the inductive and capacitive reactances are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign, the combined impedance $j\omega L + 1/(j\omega C)$ is zero, and no voltage appears across the LC branch regardless of the current delivered by the source. The width of the null in the frequency domain is again governed by $Q$: small $R$ (large $Q$) produces a sharp, narrow notch; large $R$ (small $Q$) produces a broad, shallow attenuation.

quality factor $Q$3.00
Applicability. The notch filter is used to reject a single, narrow-band interferer of known frequency while passing the surrounding spectrum with minimal attenuation. The canonical application in bioinstrumentation is the removal of $50$ Hz or $60$ Hz mains contamination from electrocardiographic, electroencephalographic, and electromyographic recordings.

05summary

Pole and zero patterns of the four filters

Each of the four filters considered above is fully specified by the positions of its poles and zeros in the complex plane. The transfer function is the rational function whose numerator vanishes at the zeros and whose denominator vanishes at the poles; the frequency response is obtained by restricting this function to the imaginary axis. The summary below collects the defining structure of each filter.

$H_{\text{LP}}(s) = \dfrac{\omega_c}{s + \omega_c}$
One real pole, no finite zero
$H_{\text{HP}}(s) = \dfrac{s}{s + \omega_c}$
One real pole, one zero at the origin
$H_{\text{BP}}(s) = \dfrac{(\omega_0/Q)\,s}{s^2 + (\omega_0/Q)\,s + \omega_0^2}$
Conjugate pole pair, one zero at the origin
$H_{\text{NT}}(s) = \dfrac{s^2 + \omega_0^2}{s^2 + (\omega_0/Q)\,s + \omega_0^2}$
Conjugate pole pair, conjugate zeros on the $j\omega$ axis

The two second-order filters (band-pass and notch) share the same denominator because they arise from the same physical network. They differ only in the location at which the output is measured, which selects a different numerator polynomial. This observation generalises: for a passive network with fixed topology, relocating the output port changes only the zeros of the transfer function, leaving the poles invariant. The pole pattern is therefore a property of the network; the zero pattern is a property of the measurement.
