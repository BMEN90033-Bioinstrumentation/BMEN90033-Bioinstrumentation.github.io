High-order filters

The first-order RC filter introduces the low-pass response but seldom meets a practical specification. Bioinstrumentation front ends for electrocardiography, pulse oximetry and electroencephalography require approximations to the ideal low-pass response that are sharper in the transition band, flatter in the passband, or deeper in the stopband than a single pole provides. This page compares five analogue low-pass designs and relates the distribution of poles and zeros in the $s$-plane to the magnitude response and to the steady-state output of each filter for a common input.

01limits of first order

Limitations of the first-order response

The elementary single-pole low-pass filter, realised as a series resistor followed by a shunt capacitor, has the transfer function

$$H_1(s) = \frac{\omega_c}{s + \omega_c}.$$

It has one pole at $s = -\omega_c$ and no finite zeros. Above the cutoff, the asymptotic attenuation is $20$ dB per decade. This rate provides little rejection when an interferer lies close to the signal band.

Additional poles increase the asymptotic attenuation to $N \cdot 20$ dB per decade for an $N^{\text{th}}$-order filter. Different pole and zero placements produce the Butterworth, Chebyshev type I, Chebyshev type II, and elliptic approximations.

Design criteria. Filter selection balances passband flatness, stopband attenuation, transition width, and phase linearity. No family optimises all four simultaneously.

02the butterworth filter

The maximally flat approximation

The Butterworth filter is defined by the requirement that the magnitude response be maximally flat at the origin: the first $2N-1$ derivatives of $|H(j\omega)|^2$ with respect to $\omega$ vanish at $\omega = 0$. The unique rational approximation satisfying this constraint places all $N$ poles on a semicircle of radius $\omega_c$ in the left half-plane, uniformly spaced by $\pi/N$ radians.

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + (\omega/\omega_c)^{2N}}$$

The transfer function contains no finite zeros and all poles are confined to the unit circle when $\omega_c = 1$. The magnitude response is strictly monotonically decreasing on $[0, \infty)$, passes through the half-power point ($-3$ dB) at $\omega = \omega_c$ for every order $N$, and exhibits an asymptotic stopband slope of $N \cdot 20$ dB per decade.

Pole placement

For normalised cutoff $\omega_c = 1$, the poles are located at

$$p_k = \exp\!\left(j\,\frac{\pi(2k + N - 1)}{2N}\right), \quad k = 1,\dots,N.$$

For $N = 4$ the poles occupy angular positions of $112.5^\circ, 157.5^\circ, 202.5^\circ$, and $247.5^\circ$, arranged symmetrically about the negative real axis and bounded away from the imaginary axis. This symmetry is a direct consequence of the flatness constraint.

Applicability. The Butterworth response is preferred when passband amplitude accuracy takes priority and no ripple can be tolerated. Typical applications include anti-aliasing prior to analogue-to-digital conversion, pre-integration smoothing, and any signal chain in which subsequent processing is sensitive to amplitude distortion within the passband. The associated cost is a comparatively shallow transition band relative to Chebyshev and elliptic designs of equivalent order.

03chebyshev type i

Equiripple approximation in the passband

If a bounded, known variation of the passband magnitude can be accepted, a substantially steeper transition band may be obtained for the same filter order. The Chebyshev type I design makes this trade: it is the rational function of order $N$ that minimises the maximum passband error subject to a monotonically decreasing stopband response. The resulting poles lie on an ellipse in the left half-plane rather than a circle.

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon^2\, T_N^2(\omega/\omega_c)}$$

Here $T_N$ denotes the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind of degree $N$, and $\varepsilon = \sqrt{10^{R_p/10} - 1}$ is chosen to yield a prescribed passband ripple $R_p$ in decibels. Because $T_N$ oscillates between $\pm 1$ on the interval $[-1, 1]$, the passband magnitude oscillates with equal ripple between unity and $1/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon^2}$. Outside the passband $T_N(\omega/\omega_c)$ grows as $2^{N-1}(\omega/\omega_c)^N$, which accounts for the steeper roll-off relative to the Butterworth response.

Pole placement

Let $v_0 = N^{-1}\sinh^{-1}(1/\varepsilon)$. The poles of the type I design are given by

$$p_k = -\sinh(v_0)\sin\theta_k + j\cosh(v_0)\cos\theta_k, \quad \theta_k = \frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2N}.$$

The locus is an ellipse with semi-minor axis $\sinh v_0$ along the real direction and semi-major axis $\cosh v_0$ along the imaginary direction. Increasing the admissible ripple decreases $v_0$, flattens the ellipse, and translates the poles toward the imaginary axis, thereby sharpening the transition band at the cost of increased passband variation.

Applicability. The Chebyshev type I response is appropriate where a specified, bounded passband ripple (typically fractions of a decibel to a few decibels) is acceptable but a gradual transition is not. It is commonly used in anti-aliasing stages whose sampling rate lies close to the signal band and in applications requiring efficient use of filter order.

04chebyshev type ii

Finite transmission zeros in the stopband

The Chebyshev type II, or inverse Chebyshev, approximation has a monotonic passband and an equiripple stopband. Finite transmission zeros on the imaginary axis produce local stopband nulls.

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \left[\varepsilon^2\, T_N^2(\omega_s/\omega)\right]^{-1}}$$

Whereas the Chebyshev polynomial in the type I response oscillates over the passband, its argument in the type II formulation is inverted so that the oscillation is mapped onto the stopband. At each zero of $T_N(\omega_s/\omega)$ the magnitude vanishes exactly, producing the transmission zeros that are observed as sharp nulls in the magnitude response.

Zero placement

$$z_k = \pm \frac{j\,\omega_s}{\cos\theta_k}, \quad \theta_k = \frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2N}, \quad k = 1,\dots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor.$$

For $N = 4$ with stopband edge $\omega_s = 1.5\,\omega_c$, the zeros are located at approximately $\pm j\,1.62\,\omega_c$ and $\pm j\,3.92\,\omega_c$, situated within the stopband so as to maximise local attenuation.

Applicability. The Chebyshev type II response is indicated when a monotonic passband is required together with a guaranteed minimum stopband attenuation, for example to suppress switching-converter noise or carrier components above the signal band. The zero frequencies $\omega_s/\cos\theta_k$ follow from the equiripple design and cannot be placed independently; rejection of a single known interferer, such as 50 or 60 Hz mains, requires a notch filter.

05the elliptic filter

Equiripple behaviour in both bands

The elliptic, or Cauer, filter is the rational approximation obtained when equiripple behaviour is permitted in both the passband and the stopband simultaneously. For a given filter order, it achieves the steepest transition band of any rational transfer function, and is optimal in the minimax sense over both bands. Its poles lie near an ellipse, as in the Chebyshev type I design, while its zeros are distributed along the imaginary axis, as in the Chebyshev type II design. Both sets of singularities are determined by the Jacobian elliptic functions, from which the family derives its name.

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon^2\, R_N^2(\omega/\omega_c,\, L)}$$

Here $R_N(\cdot, L)$ denotes the elliptic rational function of degree $N$, parameterised by a selectivity factor $L$ that fixes the ratio of stopband to passband edge frequencies. For the design illustrated here ($N = 4$, $R_p = 1$ dB, $R_s = 40$ dB), the response falls from $-1$ dB at $\omega_c$ to $-40$ dB at approximately $1.52\,\omega_c$; a Butterworth filter of the same order attenuates by only about $15$ dB at this frequency.

Singularity structure

  • Four poles near an ellipse, generating the passband ripple; the pair at $-0.105 \pm j0.994$ lies close to the imaginary axis.
  • Two conjugate pairs of zeros on the imaginary axis, each within the stopband and responsible for local nulls that bound the stopband ripple.
  • The elliptic rational function satisfies $R_N(L/x) \propto 1/R_N(x)$, where $L = \omega_s/\omega_c$ ($\approx 1.52$ here). The transmission zeros therefore lie at $L\omega_c^2/\omega_k$, where $\omega_k$ are the passband frequencies of 0 dB gain.
Applicability. Elliptic filters provide the narrowest transition for a specified order, passband ripple, and stopband attenuation. Their phase is strongly nonlinear near the band edges. Clinical electrocardiography and other applications that preserve waveform morphology generally require a more linear phase response.
