Amplifier bandwidth

Every real amplifier has a finite bandwidth. A single resistor and capacitor at the output of an ideal gain stage model this limit. This page derives the resulting transfer function, represents its denominator on the complex plane, builds the Bode magnitude plot, and relates gain to bandwidth through the gain-bandwidth product of an op-amp.

01the RC model

Deriving the transfer function

An ideal amplifier multiplies its input by a constant gain $A_v$ at all frequencies. In practice, parasitic capacitance from transistors, wiring and the load shunts high-frequency signals to ground. The model represents this with a series resistor $R$ and a shunt capacitor $C$ at the output.

The capacitor impedance is $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$, which falls as $\omega$ rises: the capacitor conducts better at high frequency and shunts the signal to ground. $R$ and $C$ form a frequency-dependent voltage divider, with the output taken across the capacitor:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{V_\text{out}}{V_\text{amp}} &= \frac{Z_C}{R + Z_C} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{j\omega C}}{R + \dfrac{1}{j\omega C}} \\[4pt] &= \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC} = \frac{1}{1 + j(\omega/\omega_0)} \end{aligned}$$

The second line follows by multiplying numerator and denominator by $j\omega C$. The product $RC$ is the time constant $\tau$, and $\omega_0 = 1/RC$ is the cutoff frequency, so the response depends only on the ratio $\omega/\omega_0$. Including the gain stage, which multiplies the input by $A_v$ before the RC network, gives the single-pole low-pass transfer function:

$$H(j\omega) = \frac{A_v}{1 + j(\omega/\omega_0)}, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{RC}$$
  • $\omega \ll \omega_0$: $j(\omega/\omega_0)$ is negligible, the denominator is close to 1 and the gain is $A_v$. The capacitor is effectively an open circuit.
  • $\omega \gg \omega_0$: the imaginary term dominates, the denominator grows and the gain falls. The capacitor shorts the output to ground.
  • $\omega = \omega_0$: the real and imaginary parts of the denominator are both 1, its magnitude is $\sqrt{2}$ and the gain is $A_v/\sqrt{2}$, the $-3\,\text{dB}$ point.
The denominator $1 + j(\omega/\omega_0)$ is a complex number whose magnitude grows with frequency. Because it divides $A_v$, the gain decreases. Section 02 represents this on the complex plane.

02the complex plane

The denominator as a phasor

The denominator $D = 1 + j(\omega/\omega_0)$ is a point in the complex plane. Its real part is fixed at 1; its imaginary part is $\omega/\omega_0$ and grows with frequency.

Raising the test frequency changes both the length and the angle of the phasor:

  • The phasor lengthens. Its magnitude is $|D| = \sqrt{1 + (\omega/\omega_0)^2}$. Since $D$ divides $A_v$, the gain falls.
  • The phasor rotates towards the imaginary axis. Its angle is $\theta = \arctan(\omega/\omega_0)$, which becomes the phase lag of the output.
  • At $\omega = \omega_0$ the imaginary part equals the real part, so $|D| = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$ and $\theta = 45^\circ$. The gain is $A_v/\sqrt{2}$, which is $3\,\text{dB}$ below the mid-band gain.
$$|H(j\omega)| = \frac{A_v}{|D|} = \frac{A_v}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega/\omega_0)^2}}$$

The audio-amplifier preset sets $R = 10\,\text{k}\Omega$, $C = 10\,\text{nF}$ and $A_v = 100$; the RF-stage preset sets $R = 500\,\Omega$, $C = 5\,\text{pF}$ and $A_v = 20$. Both place the test frequency at $\omega_0$.

The dashed arc has unit radius. The phasor tip always lies on the vertical line $\mathrm{Re} = 1$, so $|D| \ge 1$ and the gain never exceeds $A_v$.

03phase lag

What the angle means in the time domain

Dividing by a complex number of angle $\theta$ rotates the result by $-\theta$. The angle of the denominator phasor therefore appears as a phase lag of the output:

$$\angle H(j\omega) = -\arctan(\omega/\omega_0)$$

The capacitor cannot charge instantaneously through the resistor. At low frequency it has time to follow the input, so the output stays in phase. At higher frequency the input changes faster than the RC network can follow, and the output falls behind.

  • $\omega \ll \omega_0$: the angle is close to $0^\circ$ and the output follows the input with almost no delay.
  • $\omega = \omega_0$: the angle is $-45^\circ$, a lag of one-eighth of a cycle.
  • $\omega \gg \omega_0$: the angle approaches $-90^\circ$ as the capacitor dominates.

The plot shows a unit-amplitude input and the output divided by $A_v$. Above $\omega_0$ the output amplitude decreases and its lag increases.

$-3\,\text{dB}$ is the half-power point: the output power, proportional to $V^2$, is half its mid-band value. It defines the bandwidth of the amplifier, the frequency range over which the gain stays within $3\,\text{dB}$ of its maximum. At the same frequency the phase lag is $45^\circ$.

04bode magnitude

Building the Bode magnitude plot

A linear plot of gain against frequency is impractical when both span several orders of magnitude. Logarithmic scales on both axes compress these ranges.

Gain is expressed in decibels, $|H|_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}|H|$: a gain of 100 is $40\,\text{dB}$, 1000 is $60\,\text{dB}$, unity is $0\,\text{dB}$, and halving the voltage is $-6\,\text{dB}$. Frequency is spaced in decades, so each axis division is a factor of 10 and 1 Hz and 1 MHz appear on the same axis with equal weight. In logarithmic form the quotient becomes a difference, and $20\log_{10}\sqrt{x} = 10\log_{10}x$:

$$|H|_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}A_v - 10\log_{10}\!\left(1 + (\omega/\omega_0)^2\right)$$

The first term is the constant mid-band gain; the second is the loss in the RC network, which grows with frequency. In each regime one part of the denominator dominates:

  • passband, $\omega \ll \omega_0$: $(\omega/\omega_0)^2 \approx 0$, so $|H|_\text{dB} \approx 20\log_{10}A_v$, a horizontal line.
  • $-3\,\text{dB}$ point, $\omega = \omega_0$: the denominator is $\sqrt{2}$ and the gain is $20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt{2}) = -3.01\,\text{dB}$ below mid-band, the half-power point.
  • roll-off, $\omega \gg \omega_0$: the 1 is negligible and $|H|_\text{dB} \approx 20\log_{10}A_v - 20\log_{10}(\omega/\omega_0)$. Each tenfold increase in $\omega$ lowers the gain by $20\,\text{dB}$, a straight line of slope $-20\,\text{dB/decade}$ characteristic of a single-pole system.
The $-20\,\text{dB/decade}$ slope holds only for a single-pole system with one RC pair. Real amplifiers often have several poles, and each additional pole adds a further $-20\,\text{dB/decade}$: two poles give $-40\,\text{dB/decade}$, three give $-60\,\text{dB/decade}$.

05open and closed loop

Setting the gain with negative feedback

Sections 01 to 04 treat the gain $A_v$ and the cutoff frequency $\omega_0$ as independent. In an op-amp circuit they are linked by the gain-bandwidth product.

An op-amp without external components has a very high open-loop gain $A_\text{OL}$, typically $10^5$ or more:

$$v_\text{out} = A_\text{OL}\,(v_+ - v_-)$$

This gain is poorly controlled, depends on temperature and varies widely between devices, so it is not used directly. A feedback network, usually two resistors, connects the output to the inverting input. This is negative feedback, and the resistor ratio sets the closed-loop gain:

$$A_{CL} = -\frac{R_2}{R_\text{in}}$$

The closed-loop gain depends only on the external resistors, provided $A_\text{OL} \gg |A_{CL}|$. This independence from the device gain is what makes op-amp circuits predictable.

Feedback exchanges the large, poorly defined open-loop gain for a smaller gain that is set by resistor values and, as section 06 shows, for a wider bandwidth.

06gain-bandwidth product

The trade between gain and bandwidth

Internal parasitic capacitance in the op-amp creates a single dominant pole, as in the RC model of section 01. The open-loop response therefore has a fixed gain-bandwidth product:

$$\text{GBP} = \left(1 + \frac{R_2}{R_\text{in}}\right)\omega_0 = \text{constant}$$

The bandwidth is set by the noise gain $1 + R_2/R_\text{in}$, not by $A_{CL}$ directly. For a non-inverting amplifier the noise gain equals $A_{CL}$; for the inverting amplifier of section 05 it equals $|A_{CL}| + 1$. In the plot, $A_v$ denotes the noise gain, so $\omega_0 = \text{GBP}/A_v$. Reducing the gain from $A_\text{OL}$ to $A_v$ by feedback increases the bandwidth by the same factor.

  • High gain, $A_v = 1000$: large amplification but low $\omega_0$, so only slow signals are amplified fully.
  • Low gain, $A_v = 10$: modest amplification, with $\omega_0$ a factor of 100 higher.
  • Unity noise gain, the voltage follower: no amplification, and $\omega_0$ equals the GBP. This is the largest bandwidth the device can reach, which is why datasheets call it the unity-gain bandwidth. An inverting amplifier with $|A_{CL}| = 1$ has a noise gain of 2 and so $\omega_0 = \text{GBP}/2$.

All closed-loop curves meet the open-loop envelope $\text{GBP}/\omega$ and cross unity gain at $\omega = \text{GBP}$. No feedback configuration produces gain above the open-loop response. Raising $A_v$ shifts the curve up and moves $\omega_0$ to the left.

A general-purpose op-amp such as the LM741 has a GBP of about $6.28\,\text{Mrad/s}$, or $1\,\text{MHz}$. At a gain of 100, $\omega_0$ is only $62.8\,\text{krad/s}$, or $10\,\text{kHz}$; at a gain of 10 it extends to $628\,\text{krad/s}$, or $100\,\text{kHz}$. An audio amplifier covering 20 Hz to 20 kHz at a gain of 50 needs a GBP of at least $6.28\,\text{Mrad/s}$.
