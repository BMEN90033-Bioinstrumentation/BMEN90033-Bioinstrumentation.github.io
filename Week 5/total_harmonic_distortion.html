Total harmonic distortion

A nonlinear amplifier driven by a pure sine wave produces an output that contains the input frequency and its integer multiples. Total harmonic distortion (THD) quantifies this added content as the ratio of the combined harmonic amplitude to the amplitude of the fundamental. This page builds a distorted waveform from its harmonics, separates it again by Fourier decomposition, and computes its THD.

01harmonics

The fundamental and its harmonics

Every periodic signal has a fundamental frequency $f_1 = 1/T_1$, the lowest frequency component, which sets its repetition rate. A pure sine wave consists of the fundamental alone and contains no harmonic distortion.

Harmonics are sinusoids at integer multiples of the fundamental: the 2nd harmonic at $2f_1$, the 3rd at $3f_1$, and so on. Each has its own amplitude $V_n$ and phase $\phi_n$, and the periodic signal is their sum:

$$v(t) = V_1 \sin(\omega_1 t) + \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} V_n \sin(n\omega_1 t + \phi_n), \qquad \omega_1 = 2\pi f_1$$

The $n$th harmonic completes exactly $n$ cycles in each period of the fundamental, so the sum repeats with period $T_1$ whatever the harmonic amplitudes. On this page all phases are zero, and harmonic amplitudes are expressed relative to $V_1$.

Even and odd harmonics. A waveform with half-wave symmetry, $v(t + T_1/2) = -v(t)$, contains only odd harmonics. Push-pull output stages (classes B and AB) treat the two half-cycles symmetrically, so even harmonics largely cancel and odd harmonics (3rd, 5th) dominate. Single-ended stages (class A) produce both even and odd harmonics.

02combining harmonics

Building a distorted waveform

Adding harmonics to the fundamental changes the shape of the waveform without changing its period. The sliders set the amplitude of each harmonic as a percentage of $V_1$.

The upper figure shows each component and their sum over three periods. The lower figure is the amplitude spectrum of the same signal: one bar per frequency component, with height $V_n/V_1$. The two figures are equivalent descriptions of one periodic signal, in the time domain and in the frequency domain.

The presets load harmonic profiles typical of each amplifier class:

  • class A: small harmonics spread across the spectrum, both even and odd;
  • class AB: dominated by the odd harmonics, 3 % at $3f_1$ and 1.5 % at $5f_1$;
  • class B: large odd harmonics, 8 % at $3f_1$ and 4 % at $5f_1$, produced by crossover distortion.
A harmonic of a few percent is difficult to see in the time-domain waveform but is clearly resolved in the spectrum. Distortion is therefore specified and measured in the frequency domain.

03fourier decomposition

Separating a waveform into sinusoids

Fourier decomposition is the reverse of the construction in section 02: it recovers the amplitude of every sinusoidal component from the distorted waveform alone.

Sinusoids at different harmonic frequencies are orthogonal over one period, so multiplying $v(t)$ by $\sin(n\omega_1 t)$ and averaging over $T_1$ removes every component except the $n$th:

$$\begin{aligned} b_n &= \frac{2}{T_1}\int_0^{T_1} v(t)\,\sin(n\omega_1 t)\,dt \\[4pt] a_n &= \frac{2}{T_1}\int_0^{T_1} v(t)\,\cos(n\omega_1 t)\,dt \end{aligned}$$

The amplitude of the $n$th harmonic is $V_n = \sqrt{a_n^2 + b_n^2}$ and its phase follows from the ratio of the two coefficients. For the zero-phase harmonics used here $a_n = 0$ and $b_n = V_n$.

The decompose control separates the present waveform into its components, each on its own line. Reset returns to the combined view.

The components are not an approximation: their sum reconstructs the periodic waveform exactly. The next section shows the same components as a frequency spectrum.

04frequency spectrum

The spectrum in decibels

In practice the decomposition is computed numerically. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) converts a block of sampled time-domain data into a spectrum, in which each component is represented by its frequency and amplitude.

Harmonic levels are usually quoted in decibels relative to the fundamental,

$$L_n = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_n}{V_1}\;\text{dB}$$

so that 10 % corresponds to $-20$ dB, 1 % to $-40$ dB and 0.1 % to $-60$ dB. The logarithmic scale shows components that differ by several orders of magnitude on one axis. The bar at $f_1$ is the fundamental, at 0 dB; bars at $2f_1$, $3f_1$ and higher are harmonics. The model contains no components above the 5th harmonic, so $6f_1$ to $8f_1$ sit at the $-60$ dB floor of the plot.

Measurement. A pure sine wave is applied to the amplifier and the spectrum of its output is recorded. Any component at a multiple of the test frequency was generated by the amplifier.

05the thd formula

Total harmonic distortion

THD is the root-sum-square of the harmonic amplitudes divided by the amplitude of the fundamental:

$$\text{THD} = \frac{\sqrt{V_2^2 + V_3^2 + V_4^2 + V_5^2 + \cdots}}{V_1} \times 100\,\%$$

The harmonics add in quadrature because they are at different frequencies: their powers add, and the numerator is the RMS value of the harmonic part of the signal. The terms are squared, so the largest harmonic dominates. A 3rd harmonic at 8 % and a 5th at 4 % give 8.9 %, not 12 %.

The model includes harmonics up to the 5th. The figure shows the fraction of the total under the square root carried by each harmonic, $V_n^2 / \sum_k V_k^2$.

The table gives a guide to the significance of a THD figure. The row containing the present value is highlighted.

THD is also quoted in decibels, $20\log_{10}(\text{THD})$, so 1 % is $-40$ dB. It describes the harmonic content for one test frequency and amplitude, and is normally specified at both.

06biosignal measurement

Consequences for biosignal measurement

Harmonic distortion changes the shape and amplitude of the measured waveform. ECG, EEG and EMG carry diagnostic information in their shape and timing, and distortion can:

  • introduce false peaks that resemble pathological waveforms;
  • smooth sharp features needed for diagnosis;
  • shift the apparent amplitude of signal components.

The output-stage classes trade distortion against efficiency. Illustrative open-loop values, without global negative feedback:

  • class A: the lowest THD, about 1 to 2 %, at about 25 % efficiency;
  • class AB: about 3 to 5 % THD at about 55 % efficiency;
  • class B: crossover distortion, about 5 to 10 % THD, at up to 78 % efficiency;
  • class D: 90 to 95 % efficiency, with a THD that depends on the output filter.

Global negative feedback reduces these figures substantially; class AB amplifiers with feedback routinely achieve THD below 0.1 %.

Measurement amplifiers require low distortion, and portable instruments also require low power consumption. A class D stage therefore needs an output filter that suppresses its switching harmonics within the measurement band.
