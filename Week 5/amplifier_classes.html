Power amplifier classes

An output stage is classified by its conduction angle, the fraction of each input cycle during which an output device conducts. Class A conducts over the whole cycle and is linear but inefficient. Class B splits the cycle between two complementary devices and is efficient but distorts near zero. Class AB adds a small standing bias to remove most of that distortion. This page compares the three circuits, their operating points, their waveforms and their power budgets.

01conduction angle

Three output stages

The class of an amplifier is set by the bias of its output devices, which fixes the conduction angle $\theta_c$: the portion of the $360^\circ$ input cycle over which each device carries current.

  • class A, $\theta_c = 360^\circ$: a single common-emitter transistor is biased in the active region by the divider $R_1$, $R_2$ and conducts for the entire cycle. It draws supply current even with no input.
  • class B, $\theta_c = 180^\circ$: a complementary NPN and PNP pair of emitter followers. The NPN conducts on the positive half-cycle and the PNP on the negative half-cycle; with no input both are off.
  • class AB, $\theta_c$ slightly above $180^\circ$: the push-pull pair with two diodes between the bases. The diodes hold each base about one junction drop from the input node, so both devices sit at the edge of conduction.

The emitter followers of classes B and AB have a voltage gain close to 1. In this model their bases are driven by a preceding stage of gain 20, drawn as the source $20\,v_\text{in}$, so the overall gain of all three circuits is about 20. The class A stage is inverting.

The animated dots show where current flows during the cycle. Their opacity follows the conducting device: in class B only one half of the pair carries current at a time, and near the zero crossing neither does.

In class AB the readouts beside each base show the base voltages one diode drop either side of the input node. Both devices carry current at the zero crossing, which is the property that removes the class B dead zone.

02bias and swing

The Q-point on the load line

With $V_{CC} = 12\,\text{V}$ and $R_C = 1.2\,\text{k}\Omega$ the collector loop constrains the transistor to the load line

$$I_C = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CE}}{R_C}$$

which runs from $V_{CE} = V_{CC}$ at cutoff to $I_C = V_{CC}/R_C = 10\,\text{mA}$ at saturation. The bias network places the quiescent point $Q$ at $(V_{CEQ}, I_{CQ})$ on this line, and the signal moves the operating point along it.

The class A output is the AC swing of the collector about $V_{CEQ}$. It reaches cutoff when the collector rises to $V_{CC}$ and saturation when $v_{CE}$ falls to $V_{CE(\text{sat})} = 0.2\,\text{V}$, so with the stage gain $A_v = 20$ the peak output swing is

$$\hat{v}_\text{out} = \min\!\left(A_v \hat{v}_\text{in},\; V_{CEQ} - V_{CE(\text{sat})},\; V_{CC} - V_{CEQ}\right)$$

A Q-point at the centre of the line gives the largest symmetric swing. Moving it towards either end shortens the swing on that side, and the output then clips asymmetrically.

Classes B and AB place $Q$ at, or just above, cutoff. Each device then swings from $Q$ towards saturation over its own half-cycle and is idle for the other half, which is the origin of their higher efficiency.

The bias slider applies to class A only. Selecting class B fixes $I_{CQ} = 0$; selecting class AB fixes $I_{CQ} = 0.5\,\text{mA}$.

03waveforms

Crossover distortion and clipping

A push-pull follower conducts only when its base-emitter junction is forward biased. With the bases joined, neither device conducts while the base voltage lies within about $\pm 0.6\,\text{V}$ of the output, and the output stays at zero.

Referred to the input through the driver gain $A = 20$, the class B dead zone is $\pm 0.6/A = \pm 30\,\text{mV}$, and the output is

$$v_\text{out} = \begin{cases} 0, & |v_\text{in}| < V_\text{dz}/A \\[4pt] \operatorname{sgn}(v_\text{in})\left(|v_\text{in}| - V_\text{dz}/A\right) A, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

with $V_\text{dz} = 0.6\,\text{V}$ for class B and $0.05\,\text{V}$ for class AB, and the result limited to $\pm(V_{CC} - V_{CE(\text{sat})})$. The flat step at each zero crossing is crossover distortion. Its size is fixed, so it is a large fraction of a small signal and a small fraction of a large one.

The class A stage has no dead zone but clips at the limits set by its Q-point, shown dashed. The measured gain below is the peak output divided by the peak input; it falls below 20 once either distortion mechanism removes part of the waveform.

The bias diodes of class AB reduce the effective dead zone from 0.6 V to 0.05 V at the bases. The step at the zero crossing is then too small to see at this scale, while the quiescent current stays small.

04power budget

Efficiency of each class

The efficiency is the fraction of the DC supply power that reaches the load as signal power; the remainder is dissipated as heat in the output devices:

$$\eta = \frac{P_L}{P_\text{DC}}, \qquad P_D = P_\text{DC} - P_L, \qquad P_L = \frac{\hat{v}_\text{out}^{\,2}}{2R_L}$$

Class A. The supply delivers $P_\text{DC} = V_{CC} I_{CQ}$ whatever the signal. With a resistive load the efficiency cannot exceed 25 %; a transformer-coupled load raises the limit to 50 %. At zero input all of $P_\text{DC}$ is dissipated in the circuit.

Class B. Each supply delivers a half-wave rectified current of peak $\hat{\imath} = \hat{v}_\text{out}/R_L$, whose average is $\hat{\imath}/\pi$, so

$$P_\text{DC} = \frac{2 V_{CC}\, \hat{\imath}}{\pi}, \qquad \eta = \frac{\pi}{4}\,\frac{\hat{v}_\text{out}}{V_{CC}} \le \frac{\pi}{4} \approx 78.5\,\%$$

Class AB. The standing bias current $I_Q$ adds to both supplies, $P_\text{DC} = 2 V_{CC}\left(\hat{\imath}/\pi + I_Q\right)$. Typical efficiencies lie between 50 % and 60 %, between those of classes A and B.

The efficiency of classes B and AB rises in proportion to the output amplitude, because the supply current is drawn only in proportion to the signal. Class A draws its full supply current at all amplitudes, so its efficiency is highest at full swing and zero at rest.
