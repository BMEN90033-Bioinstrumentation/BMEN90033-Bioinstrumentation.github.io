Power amplifier classes

An output stage is classified by its conduction angle, the fraction of each input cycle during which an output device conducts. Class A conducts over the whole cycle and is linear but inefficient. Class B splits the cycle between two complementary devices and is efficient but distorts near zero. Class AB biases the two devices towards conduction to remove most of that distortion. This page compares the three circuits, their operating points, their waveforms and their power budgets.

01conduction angle

Three output stages

The class of an amplifier is set by the bias of its output devices, which fixes the conduction angle $\theta_c$: the portion of the $360^\circ$ input cycle over which each device carries current.

  • class A, $\theta_c = 360^\circ$: a single common-emitter transistor is biased in the active region by the divider $R_1$, $R_2$ and conducts for the entire cycle. It draws supply current even with no input.
  • class B, $\theta_c \approx 180^\circ$: a complementary NPN and PNP pair of emitter followers with joined bases. The NPN conducts on the positive half-cycle and the PNP on the negative half-cycle; with no input both are off. Each device needs $V_{BE(\text{on})} = 0.6\,\text{V}$ before it conducts, so its conduction angle falls short of $180^\circ$ by the dead zone about each zero crossing.
  • class AB, $\theta_c$ at or slightly above $180^\circ$: the push-pull pair with two diodes between the bases, fed by the bias current $I_\text{bias}$ through $R_{b1}$ and $R_{b2}$. Each diode lifts its base by its forward drop $V_D$, so the dead zone shrinks to $V_{BE(\text{on})} - V_D$ and vanishes when $V_D = V_{BE(\text{on})}$.

The emitter followers of classes B and AB have a voltage gain close to 1. In this model their bases are driven by a preceding stage of gain 20, drawn as the source $20\,v_\text{in}$, so the overall gain of all three circuits is about 20. The class A stage is inverting.

The animated dots show where current flows during the cycle. Their opacity follows the conducting device: in class B only one half of the pair carries current at a time, and near the zero crossing neither does.

In class AB the readouts beside each base give $V_B = 20\,v_\text{in} \pm V_D$, the driver output one diode drop either side of the input node, and the base current $I_C/h_{FE}$ with $h_{FE} = 100$. The model treats each output device as off below $V_{BE(\text{on})}$, and the diode drop $V_D$ is set directly by the slider rather than derived from a diode law.

02bias and swing

The Q-point on the load line

With $V_{CC} = 12\,\text{V}$, $R_C = 1.2\,\text{k}\Omega$ and $R_E = 200\,\Omega$, and taking $I_E \approx I_C$, the DC collector loop constrains the class A transistor to the DC load line

$$I_C = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CE}}{R_C + R_E}, \qquad V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} - I_{CQ}\left(R_C + R_E\right)$$

which runs from $V_{CE} = V_{CC}$ at cutoff to $I_C = V_{CC}/(R_C + R_E) = 8.57\,\text{mA}$ at saturation. The divider $R_1$, $R_2$ places the quiescent point $Q$ at $(V_{CEQ}, I_{CQ})$ on this line. It is designed with $10\,I_B$ in $R_2$, so $R_1$ carries $11\,I_B$ with $I_B = I_{CQ}/h_{FE}$ and $h_{FE} = 100$.

At signal frequencies $C_E$ bypasses $R_E$, so the signal moves the operating point along the AC load line through $Q$ with slope $-1/R_C$. The collector can rise by $I_{CQ} R_C$ before the transistor cuts off and fall by $V_{CEQ} - V_{CE(\text{sat})}$, with $V_{CE(\text{sat})} = 0.2\,\text{V}$, before it saturates. With the stage gain $A_v = 20$ the peak undistorted output is

$$\hat{v}_\text{out} = \min\!\left(A_v \hat{v}_\text{in},\; V_{CEQ} - V_{CE(\text{sat})},\; I_{CQ} R_C\right)$$

The largest symmetric swing occurs when the two limits are equal, $I_{CQ} = (V_{CC} - V_{CE(\text{sat})})/(2R_C + R_E) = 4.54\,\text{mA}$. Moving the Q-point either way shortens the swing on one side, and the output then clips asymmetrically.

Classes B and AB idle at cutoff, $I_{CQ} = 0$. Each device swings along its own load line, $I_C = (V_{CC} - V_{CE})/R_L$, towards saturation over its half-cycle and is idle for the other half, which is the origin of their higher efficiency.

The bias slider applies to class A only. The readout gives the divider resistances that set the selected Q-point.

03waveforms

Crossover distortion and clipping

A push-pull follower conducts only when its base-emitter junction is forward biased. With the bases joined, neither device conducts while the base voltage lies within about $\pm 0.6\,\text{V}$ of the output, and the output stays at zero.

With the driver output $v_\text{drv} = A\,v_\text{in}$, $A = 20$, the output is

$$v_\text{out} = \begin{cases} 0, & |v_\text{drv}| \le V_\text{dz} \\[4pt] \operatorname{sgn}(v_\text{drv})\left(|v_\text{drv}| - V_\text{dz}\right), & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
$$V_\text{dz} = \begin{cases} V_{BE(\text{on})}, & \text{class B} \\[2pt] \max\left(0,\, V_{BE(\text{on})} - V_D\right), & \text{class AB} \end{cases}$$

with $V_{BE(\text{on})} = 0.6\,\text{V}$, so the class B dead zone referred to the input is $\pm 0.6/A = \pm 30\,\text{mV}$. The result is limited to $\pm(V_{CC} - V_{CE(\text{sat})})$. The flat step at each zero crossing is crossover distortion. Its size is fixed, so it is a large fraction of a small signal and a small fraction of a large one.

The class A stage has no dead zone but clips at the limits set by its Q-point, shown dashed. The measured gain below is the peak output divided by the peak input; it falls below 20 once either distortion mechanism removes part of the waveform.

The bias diodes of class AB shrink the dead zone to $V_{BE(\text{on})} - V_D$. The diode drop slider shows the residual step at the zero crossing narrowing to nothing as $V_D$ approaches $V_{BE(\text{on})}$. Real designs set $V_D$ slightly above $V_{BE(\text{on})}$ and add small emitter resistors to fix a stable quiescent current; that region is outside this model.

04power budget

Efficiency of each class

The efficiency is the fraction of the DC supply power that reaches the load as signal power; the remainder is dissipated as heat in the output devices:

$$\eta = \frac{P_L}{P_\text{DC}}, \qquad P_D = P_\text{DC} - P_L, \qquad P_L = \frac{\hat{v}_\text{out}^{\,2}}{2R_L}$$

Class A. The supply delivers the collector current and the current in $R_1$ whatever the signal, $P_\text{DC} = V_{CC}\left(I_{CQ} + I_{R1}\right)$ with $I_{R1} = 11\,I_{CQ}/h_{FE}$. With the collector resistor as load the efficiency cannot exceed 25 %; the divider current and the drop across $R_E$ lower the achievable value to about 20 % here. A transformer-coupled load raises the ideal limit to 50 %. At zero input all of $P_\text{DC}$ is dissipated in the circuit.

Class B. Each supply delivers a half-wave rectified current of peak $\hat{\imath} = \hat{v}_\text{out}/R_L$, whose average is $\hat{\imath}/\pi$, so

$$P_\text{DC} = \frac{2 V_{CC}\, \hat{\imath}}{\pi}, \qquad \eta = \frac{\pi}{4}\,\frac{\hat{v}_\text{out}}{V_{CC}} \le \frac{\pi}{4} \approx 78.5\,\%$$

Class AB. The bias current $I_\text{bias} = 0.5\,\text{mA}$ flows from $+V_{CC}$ to $-V_{CC}$ through $R_{b1}$, the diodes and $R_{b2}$ at all times, so $P_\text{DC} = 2 V_{CC}\left(\hat{\imath}/\pi + I_\text{bias}\right)$. Its efficiency therefore lies below that of class B, by an amount that is largest at low output. Practical class AB stages also carry a quiescent collector current and typically reach 50 % to 60 %.

The efficiency of classes B and AB rises in proportion to the output amplitude, because the supply current is drawn only in proportion to the signal. Class A draws its full supply current at all amplitudes, so its efficiency is highest at full swing and zero at rest.
