The class D amplifier

A class D amplifier switches its output transistors fully on or fully off at high frequency, unlike class A, B and AB stages, which operate in the linear region. The input is encoded as a pulse-width modulated (PWM) waveform, amplified by a MOSFET half-bridge and recovered by a low-pass filter, with typical efficiencies of 90 to 95 %. This page follows the signal through each stage of that chain.

01the input signal

Linear and switching amplification

The input is an analogue sine wave representing an audio or sensor signal. In a linear amplifier this signal controls the transistor current continuously. In a class D amplifier it is only the reference for a switching process.

A linear output device carries load current while a substantial voltage appears across it, so it dissipates $V_{CE}\,I_C$ throughout the cycle. A switch is either open, with no current, or closed, with almost no voltage across it. In both states the product of voltage and current is small, and so is the dissipation.

The input is described relative to the peak $\hat{v}_c$ of the triangle carrier introduced in the next section. The ratio is the modulation index

$$m = \frac{\hat{v}_\text{in}}{\hat{v}_c}, \qquad 0 \le m \le 1$$

which sets how far the duty cycle of the PWM waveform swings either side of 50 %.

The amplitude slider sets $m$ as a percentage. For $m > 1$ the input would exceed the carrier peak, the comparator output would stop switching near the signal peaks, and the output would clip.

02pulse-width modulation

The comparator generates PWM

A comparator receives the input signal on its non-inverting terminal and a high-frequency triangle carrier on its inverting terminal. Its output is HIGH whenever the input lies above the carrier and LOW whenever it lies below.

The result is a pulse-width modulated signal: a rectangular wave at the carrier frequency $f_\text{sw}$ whose duty cycle follows the input. Over one carrier period the triangle sweeps linearly between $-\hat{v}_c$ and $+\hat{v}_c$, so the fraction of time spent above it is

$$D = \frac{v_\text{in} + \hat{v}_c}{2\,\hat{v}_c}$$

When the input is at its peak the output is HIGH for most of each carrier period; at its trough the output is LOW for most of the period; at zero the duty cycle is 50 %. The average of the PWM waveform over one carrier period is therefore a linear function of the input.

The carrier must be much faster than the signal, $f_\text{sw} \gg f_\text{sig}$, so that the input is nearly constant within each carrier period. The model uses $f_\text{sig} = 1\,\text{kHz}$ and the ratio slider sets $f_\text{sw}/f_\text{sig}$ between 5 and 60, so $f_\text{sw}$ runs from 5 to 60 kHz. These frequencies are lower than in practice so that individual pulses remain visible.

03the switching stage

The MOSFET half-bridge

The PWM signal drives a complementary pair of MOSFETs, a PMOS above and an NMOS below, arranged as a half-bridge between $V_{CC}$ and ground. The devices act as switches: each is either fully on or fully off.

A gate driver applies the inverted signal $\overline{\text{PWM}}$ to both gates. When PWM is HIGH the gates are low, the PMOS conducts and the switching node is connected to $V_{CC}$. When PWM is LOW the gates are high, the NMOS conducts and the node is connected to ground. The switching node $v_\text{sw}$ is therefore a copy of the PWM waveform with amplitude $V_{CC} = 12\,\text{V}$.

The power dissipated in a switch is the product of the voltage across it and the current through it. When off, $I_D$ is negligible; when on, $V_{DS} = I_D R_{DS(\text{on})}$ is small. The losses that remain are modelled as

$$\begin{aligned} P_\text{cond} &= I_{L,\text{rms}}^2\, R_{DS(\text{on})} \\[4pt] P_\text{sw} &= f_\text{sw}\, V_{CC}\, \overline{|i_L|}\, \frac{t_r + t_f}{2} \\[4pt] P_\text{drv} &= 2\, Q_g V_{GS} f_\text{sw} + V_{CC} I_Q \end{aligned}$$

The inductor current $i_L$ always flows through one of the two devices, so the conduction loss uses its RMS value. The switching loss arises because each device passes through its linear region during every edge, while both voltage and current are significant; $\overline{|i_L|}$ is the mean current at the edges. The drive term charges both gates once per period and supplies the quiescent current of the comparator and driver.

  • $R_{DS(\text{on})} = 0.1\,\Omega$ per device, $t_r = t_f = 20\,\text{ns}$;
  • $Q_g = 10\,\text{nC}$ per device at $V_{GS} = 12\,\text{V}$, $I_Q = 1\,\text{mA}$.
These are representative values for a small power MOSFET pair and driver, not a specific part. The switching and gate-drive losses grow in proportion to $f_\text{sw}$, which limits how high the switching frequency can be raised.

04output filter

The LC low-pass filter recovers the signal

The switching node is a high-frequency rectangular wave between $V_{CC}$ and ground. A second-order LC low-pass filter between this node and the load removes the switching frequency and passes its local average, which follows the input.

While PWM is HIGH, current flows from the supply through the inductor into the capacitor and load. While PWM is LOW, the energy stored in the inductor maintains the current. The filter node settles at $D\,V_{CC}$, which varies with the input, with a residual ripple at $f_\text{sw}$. With the load $R_L = 8\,\Omega$ across $C$, the filter response is

$$H(f) = \frac{1}{1 - (f/f_c)^2 + j\,f/(f_c Q)}$$ $$f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad Q = R_L\sqrt{C/L}$$

With $L = 600\,\mu\text{H}$ and $C = 4.7\,\mu\text{F}$, $f_c = 3.0\,\text{kHz}$ and $Q = 0.71$, a Butterworth response that satisfies $f_\text{sig} < f_c < f_\text{sw}$. Above $f_c$ the filter attenuates at $-40$ dB per decade. The fundamental of the switching waveform has peak $2V_{CC}/\pi$ at 50 % duty, so the ripple at the output is approximately

$$\hat{v}_\text{ripple} \approx \frac{2 V_{CC}}{\pi}\,\left|H(f_\text{sw})\right|$$

The plotted output is not computed from this estimate: the circuit equations of the filter, the blocking capacitor and the load are integrated in time with the PWM waveform as input.

The half-bridge node idles at $V_{CC}/2$, so the DC-blocking capacitor $C_B = 470\,\mu\text{F}$ in series with the load removes that DC component; with $R_L$ it forms a high-pass filter at $1/(2\pi R_L C_B) = 42\,\text{Hz}$. Ratios of 5 to 10 leave visible ripple; practical audio designs place $f_\text{sw}$ at several hundred kilohertz.

05complete signal chain

The full amplifier and its efficiency

The complete amplifier joins the comparator, the half-bridge and the LC filter. The comparator inputs are reversed relative to section 02, so its output is $\overline{\text{PWM}}$, the gate drive required for the switching node to follow PWM.

The three traces show the signal at each stage: the analogue input, the two-level switching node and the load voltage. After $C_B$ the load voltage is centred on zero, with a signal peak close to $m\,V_{CC}/2\,|H(f_\text{sig})|$. The power budget follows from the simulated currents:

$$P_\text{DC} = P_L + P_\text{rip} + P_\text{cond} + P_\text{sw} + P_\text{drv}, \qquad \eta = \frac{P_L}{P_\text{DC}}$$

Here $P_L$ is the load power at the signal frequency and $P_\text{rip}$ the switching ripple dissipated in the load, which does no useful work. Near full modulation the efficiency exceeds 95 %. At low modulation the fixed drive and quiescent power and the ripple current dominate, and the efficiency falls.

For comparison, the linear output stages reach about 25 % (class A), 55 % (class AB) and 78 % (class B). Practical class D amplifiers reach 90 to 95 % because the output devices do not dwell in the linear region. The model gives a higher figure at full modulation because it omits inductor resistance, core loss and dead-time loss.

06device choice

Why MOSFETs

Class D operation requires fast switching with low conduction and drive losses. MOSFETs are generally preferred to BJTs for four reasons.

  • switching speed: a saturated BJT has a storage time, because minority carriers in the base must be removed before it turns off. MOSFETs are majority-carrier devices with no storage time and switch faster.
  • voltage-driven gate: a BJT needs continuous base current to stay on. A MOSFET needs only its gate capacitance charged, after which no steady-state gate current flows, which simplifies the driver.
  • low on-resistance: power MOSFETs reach $R_{DS(\text{on})}$ in the low milliohm range. At high currents this gives a smaller voltage drop than the $V_{CE(\text{sat})}$ of a BJT, and therefore lower conduction loss.
  • applications: audio amplifiers, motor drivers, DC-DC converters and battery-powered biomedical devices.
Class D is the dominant amplifier topology in portable and battery-powered biomedical devices. Efficiency determines battery life and the heat generated, both of which constrain wearable and implantable systems.
