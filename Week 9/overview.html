Comparators and timers in practice

A comparator reduces a continuous voltage to a binary decision against a fixed reference. A timer converts an interval into a signal: either a single pulse of chosen duration, or a square wave at a chosen rate. Together these two blocks form the interface between the analogue front end and the digital domain, and appear in most measurement instruments and stimulators. The sections below illustrate six standard uses; the underlying circuits and design equations are derived in the pages that follow.

01battery low-voltage cutoff

Protecting a lithium cell from over-discharge

A lithium-ion cell suffers permanent damage when it is discharged below approximately $3.0\,\mathrm{V}$. Wearable monitors, implanted pumps, and portable defibrillators therefore include a dedicated comparator whose role is to disconnect the load before this limit is reached.

The first panel plots the cell voltage as the cell discharges under load. A comparator continuously evaluates whether $V_\mathrm{cell}$ exceeds the cutoff $V_\mathrm{cut}$ and drives a single binary output to a power-path switch. While the cell voltage remains above the threshold the load stays connected; once it falls below, the comparator output changes state and the switch opens.

Drag the dashed threshold line to change the cutoff voltage and observe the instant of disconnection.

Practical implementations add a small amount of hysteresis around the threshold so that the comparator output does not chatter on a noisy supply. A comparator with hysteresis is a Schmitt trigger, covered on the next page.

02heart-rate detection

Counting R-peaks in a continuous ECG

A bedside heart-rate monitor reports beats per minute, whereas its input is a continuous analogue ECG. A comparator converts the waveform into discrete beat events. With its threshold placed above the T-wave and below the R-peak, the comparator emits one digital pulse on every upward crossing, and a downstream counter divides the count by the window length to obtain the rate.

Drag the threshold line to set the level at which the comparator switches. If the threshold is too low, T-waves and noise are counted as beats; if it is too high, R-peaks are missed. The same arrangement appears in pulse oximeters, oscillometric blood-pressure cuffs, and event-driven sleep monitors.

A short refractory window follows each pulse to suppress further crossings, since the R-peak spans more than one sample and would otherwise produce several pulses per beat.

03ambient light switch

Switching a streetlight at dusk

A photoresistor or photodiode produces a voltage proportional to incident light. A comparator placed at its output reduces the continuous reading to a binary day/night decision. The same circuit appears in automatic headlights, in the ambient-light sensor of a phone display, and in the standby logic of pulse-oximetry probes that sample only when a finger covers the lens.

Drag the sun across the sky to vary the sensor voltage. When it falls below the dusk threshold, the comparator output goes high and the lamp switches on. The threshold marker on the right-hand bar sets the light level below which the lamp is switched on.

Without hysteresis, a passing cloud near the threshold would cause the lamp to flicker. A practical photo-switch uses a Schmitt-trigger comparator with separate on and off thresholds, so that the lamp switches once at dusk and once at dawn.

04cardiac pacing

Generating timed stimulation pulses

An implanted pacemaker delivers a brief electrical pulse to the heart at a controlled rate. The timer determines the instant of each pulse, emitting one pulse of fixed width each time its internal interval expires. Pulse amplitude is set by a separate output stage; the timer sets only the timing.

The same building block appears in transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulators (TENS), in cochlear implants that drive electrode arrays in synchrony, and in retinal prostheses. In each case the clinician selects a rate and a pulse width, and the timer generates the corresponding periodic waveform.

The sliders set the pacing rate and the pulse width. Each pulse corresponds to one paced heartbeat.

A timer that produces a continuous train of pulses is an astable multivibrator. A timer that emits a single pulse on demand and then waits is a monostable multivibrator. Both are constructed from a comparator with a charging capacitor; the design is derived later in the week.

05pulse-width modulation

Setting average power with a duty cycle

A timer that produces a square wave at fixed frequency but adjustable on-time can regulate average power without dissipating heat in a series resistance. The load sees the full supply during the on-interval of each cycle and zero during the off-interval, so its time-average is the supply voltage scaled by the duty cycle $D$.

The same technique dims a phototherapy LED panel without colour shift, sets the motor speed of a syringe pump, and regulates the backlight of a clinical display. Class-D audio amplifiers apply it at a much higher carrier frequency to drive a loudspeaker.

The duty-cycle slider sets $D$; the load brightness follows the time-average of the square wave. The second plot shows the steady-state envelope of the filtered bulb voltage against PWM frequency: the green line is the time-average $D \cdot V_{cc}$, which is independent of frequency, and the shaded band is the peak-to-peak ripple at the bulb, modelled as a first-order thermal load with $\tau = 50\,\mathrm{ms}$. The bulb graphic is driven by the same filtered waveform.

Below the thermal corner frequency of the bulb the ripple band is wide and the flicker is visible. Above the corner the band converges on the average and the output appears steady. LED flicker is not perceived above roughly $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$, motor inertia smooths ripple above a few hundred hertz, and a class-D amplifier switches at hundreds of kilohertz so that only the slow envelope reaches the loudspeaker.

06switch debouncing

Filtering a bouncing contact with an $RC$ network and a Schmitt trigger

When a mechanical key is pressed, the metal contacts do not close cleanly. They strike, separate, and continue to bounce for several milliseconds before settling. A digital input registers every closure as a separate keypress, so one physical press produces a burst of phantom characters.

The standard remedy is an $RC$ low-pass feeding a Schmitt-trigger comparator. The capacitor voltage obeys $\tau\,\dot V_C = V_\mathrm{in} - V_C$ with $\tau = RC$, so $V_C$ cannot follow the bouncing input directly and instead integrates the burst. The Schmitt output goes high when $V_C$ crosses the upper threshold $V_{T+}$ and resets only after $V_C$ falls below the lower threshold $V_{T-}$. Choosing $\tau$ longer than the bounce interval guarantees that $V_C$ crosses $V_{T+}$ exactly once per physical press.

Click the input box and type. Each keypress is replaced by a simulated burst of contact bounces. The lower trace shows $V_C(t)$ integrating that burst, with the dashed band marking the Schmitt hysteresis window. Every $\mathrm{LOW}\!\to\!\mathrm{HIGH}$ transition of the Schmitt output emits one character; the marker above each transition is green for the intended press and violet for a phantom. At $T_d = 0$ the capacitor voltage follows the bounces almost exactly and many phantoms appear; increasing $T_d$ (which sets $\tau = T_d$) smooths $V_C$ and removes them.

A typical key bounces for $1$ to $5\,\mathrm{ms}$. A time constant near $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ filters the bounces while remaining well below the inter-key interval of a fast typist (approximately $80\,\mathrm{ms}$). If $T_d$ is too short, residual phantoms remain; if it is too long, genuine presses repeated in quick succession are suppressed.
