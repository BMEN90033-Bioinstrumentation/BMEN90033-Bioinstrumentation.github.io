Inside the bipolar junction transistor

A bipolar junction transistor is two pn junctions that share a thin, lightly doped middle region. Forward bias on one junction injects carriers into that region, and the reverse-biased second junction collects almost all of them. This page follows the carriers through the device: the doping structure, the equilibrium and injected carrier concentrations, and the bias conditions that define each region of operation.

01the three-layer structure

Emitter, base and collector

In the NPN transistor an n-type emitter and an n-type collector enclose a p-type base. The PNP transistor reverses every doping type, so holes take the role of electrons. The three regions are doped very differently:

  • emitter: heavily doped, $N_E \approx 10^{19}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, so that it supplies the carriers injected into the base;
  • base: thin and lightly doped, $N_B \approx 10^{17}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, so that few injected carriers recombine while crossing it;
  • collector: the most lightly doped, $N_C \approx 10^{15}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, so that its junction sustains a wide depletion region and a high reverse voltage.

Each junction carries a depletion region of fixed, ionised dopants: positive donor ions on the n side and negative acceptor ions on the p side. Their charge sets up a built-in field, and at equilibrium the drift it causes balances the diffusion of carriers across the junction. For an abrupt junction the depletion width is

$$W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{q}\,\frac{N_A + N_D}{N_A N_D}\,\left(V_{bi} - V\right)}$$

where $V$ is the applied forward voltage. Forward bias narrows the region and reverse bias widens it, and the region extends further into the more lightly doped side.

The emitter and collector of an NPN transistor are both n-type but are not interchangeable. The much higher emitter doping sets the efficiency of injection, and a transistor operated with the two terminals exchanged has a far lower current gain.

02carrier concentrations

Majority and minority carriers

At equilibrium the electron and hole concentrations in each region obey the mass-action law,

$$n\,p = n_i^2, \qquad n_i \approx 1.5 \times 10^{10}\,\text{cm}^{-3}\ \text{(silicon, 300 K)}$$

With every dopant ionised, the majority concentration equals the doping and the minority concentration follows from the law. For the NPN device:

$$\begin{aligned} p_{E0} &= n_i^2 / N_E \approx 22\ \text{cm}^{-3} \\ n_{B0} &= n_i^2 / N_B \approx 2.3 \times 10^{3}\ \text{cm}^{-3} \\ p_{C0} &= n_i^2 / N_C \approx 2.3 \times 10^{5}\ \text{cm}^{-3} \end{aligned}$$

Thermal generation therefore leaves a small but non-zero minority population even in the heavily doped emitter. Forward bias on the base-emitter junction raises the minority concentration at the base edge of that junction exponentially,

$$n_B(0) = n_{B0}\, e^{V_{BE}/V_T}, \qquad V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \approx 25.9\,\text{mV at 300 K}$$

The model keeps this injected density at least a decade below the base doping, which is the low-injection condition. The collector junction sets the density at the other edge of the base, and in a base much thinner than the diffusion length the profile is a straight line between the two edge values:

$$n_B(W_B) = n_{B0}\, e^{V_{BC}/V_T}, \qquad I_C \approx \frac{q A D_n}{W_B}\,\bigl[n_B(0) - n_B(W_B)\bigr]$$

In the active region the collector junction is reverse biased (the model uses $V_{BC} = -2\,\text{V}$), so $n_B(W_B)$ is effectively zero and the density falls linearly to zero across the base. Its gradient drives the diffusion current that becomes the collector current. On the logarithmic axis this linear fall appears as a sharp drop at the collector edge. In saturation both junctions are forward biased, both edge densities are raised, and the base holds the excess stored charge. With no bias at all, both edges sit at $n_{B0}$ and the profile is flat.

On the linear axis the minority concentrations, up to 18 orders of magnitude below the emitter doping, cannot be distinguished from zero; only the logarithmic axis shows them.

03operating regions

Bias and the regions of operation

Each junction may be forward or reverse biased, and the combination sets the region of operation:

  • cutoff: neither junction forward biased; no carriers are injected and no collector current flows;
  • forward active: base-emitter forward, base-collector reverse; the transistor amplifies;
  • saturation: both junctions forward biased; $V_{CE}$ falls to $V_{CE(\text{sat})} \approx 0.2\,\text{V}$;
  • reverse active: the active region with the junction roles exchanged, rarely used and not shown here.

In the forward-active region the collector current depends exponentially on the base-emitter voltage and is a fixed multiple of the base current:

$$I_C \approx I_S\, e^{V_{BE}/V_T}, \qquad I_C = \beta I_B$$ $$I_E = I_C + I_B, \qquad \alpha = \frac{I_C}{I_E} = \frac{\beta}{\beta + 1}$$

The gain $\beta$ (the $h_{FE}$ of datasheets) is large for two reasons. The emitter is far more heavily doped than the base, so almost all of the emitter current consists of carriers injected into the base rather than carriers flowing back from it. The base is thin compared with the minority-carrier diffusion length, so few injected carriers recombine before reaching the collector.
