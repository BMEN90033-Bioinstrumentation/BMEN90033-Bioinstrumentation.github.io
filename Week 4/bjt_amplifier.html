The BJT as an amplifier

A bipolar junction transistor biased in the forward-active region converts a small change in base-emitter voltage into a much larger change in collector current, and a collector resistor turns that current into an amplified voltage. This page builds the fixed-bias common-emitter amplifier from its DC operating point, linearises the exponential junction characteristic about that point, and derives the voltage gain and its sign.

The fixed-bias common-emitter amplifier

The emitter is common to the input and output loops. A DC source $V_{BB}$ drives the base through $R_B$ and sets the operating point, and the signal $v_\text{in}(t)$ is added in series with it. The collector is supplied from $V_{CC}$ through $R_C$, and the output is the collector-emitter voltage $v_{CE}$.

Kirchhoff's voltage law around the two loops gives

$$V_{BB} + v_\text{in}(t) = i_B R_B + v_{BE}, \qquad V_{CC} = i_C R_C + v_{CE}$$

The model uses silicon values for an NPN transistor:

  • $V_{BE(\text{on})} = 0.7\,\text{V}$, the junction voltage below which the bias is taken as cutoff;
  • $V_{CE(\text{sat})} = 0.2\,\text{V}$, the residual collector-emitter voltage in saturation;
  • $I_S = 10^{-14}\,\text{A}$ and $V_T = kT/q = 26\,\text{mV}$, the parameters of the exponential junction law used throughout;
  • $h_{FE} = \beta$, the forward current gain, set independently; the Early effect is neglected.
Here the signal shares the base loop with the bias source. A practical stage usually couples the signal through a capacitor and sets the bias with a resistive divider and an emitter resistor, which makes the operating point much less dependent on $\beta$.

02dc bias

The quiescent point and the load line

With $v_\text{in} = 0$ the base loop fixes the quiescent base current. The junction obeys the exponential law $I_C = I_S\, e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, so $V_{BE}$ is found numerically, by Newton's method, from

$$V_{BB} = \frac{I_S\, e^{V_{BE}/V_T}}{\beta}\, R_B + V_{BE}$$

The quiescent currents and voltage then follow:

$$I_{BQ} = \frac{V_{BB} - V_{BE}}{R_B}, \qquad I_{CQ} = \beta I_{BQ}$$ $$V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} - I_{CQ} R_C$$

The collector loop restricts the operating point to the DC load line, from $V_{CE} = V_{CC}$ at $I_C = 0$ to $I_C = V_{CC}/R_C$ at $V_{CE} = 0$. The Q-point is the intersection of the load line with the characteristic for $I_{BQ}$. Three cases arise:

  • cutoff: $V_{BB} < V_{BE(\text{on})}$, so $I_{CQ} = 0$ and $V_{CEQ} = V_{CC}$;
  • forward active: $\beta I_{BQ} \le I_{C(\text{sat})} = (V_{CC} - V_{CE(\text{sat})})/R_C$;
  • saturation: $\beta I_{BQ}$ exceeds $I_{C(\text{sat})}$, the collector current is held at $I_{C(\text{sat})}$, and the ratio $I_{CQ}/I_{BQ}$, the forced beta, falls below $\beta$. The bracket on the plot shows the gap.

The output can swing furthest in both directions when the Q-point lies near the middle of the active part of the load line, with $V_{CEQ}$ close to $V_{CC}/2$.

Fixed bias sets $I_{BQ}$, so $I_{CQ}$ is proportional to $\beta$. Because $\beta$ varies widely between devices, a design that is centred for one transistor can be in saturation with another. Raising $h_{FE}$ moves the Q-point up the load line towards saturation.

03small-signal operation

Linearising the exponential

A signal $v_{be}$ superimposed on $V_{BEQ}$ changes the collector current through the exponential law. About the Q-point,

$$i_C = I_{CQ}\, e^{v_{be}/V_T} \approx I_{CQ}\left(1 + \frac{v_{be}}{V_T}\right) \qquad \text{for } |v_{be}| \ll V_T$$

The linear term defines the transconductance, the slope of the tangent at Q, and with it the small-signal resistances seen at the emitter and the base:

$$g_m = \left.\frac{\partial i_C}{\partial v_{BE}}\right|_Q = \frac{I_{CQ}}{V_T}, \qquad r_e = \frac{1}{g_m}, \qquad r_\pi = \frac{\beta}{g_m}$$

Only part of $v_\text{in}$ reaches the junction, because $R_B$ and $r_\pi$ form a divider:

$$v_{be} = v_\text{in}\,\frac{r_\pi}{R_B + r_\pi}$$

The figure projects the input $v_{BE}(t)$, drawn below the curve with time running downward, through the characteristic to the output $i_C(t)$ on the right. The exact response follows the curve; the linear response follows the tangent. Because the exponential bends upward, positive half-cycles of the exact output are larger than negative ones, and the two responses separate as the amplitude grows. Large inputs also clip at $I_{C(\text{sat})}$ and at cutoff.

The tangent approximation holds while $|v_{be}|$ is small compared with $V_T \approx 26\,\text{mV}$. Raising the input amplitude, or moving the Q-point with $V_{BB}$, shows where the linear model fails.

04voltage gain

Voltage gain and phase inversion

The signal component of the collector current flows through $R_C$, so the output changes by $v_{ce} = -i_c R_C$. Combining this with $i_c = g_m v_{be}$ and the input divider gives the voltage gain:

$$A_v = \frac{v_{ce}}{v_\text{in}} = -g_m R_C\,\frac{r_\pi}{R_B + r_\pi} = -\frac{\beta R_C}{R_B + r_\pi}$$

The negative sign is the phase inversion of the common-emitter stage: a rise in $v_\text{in}$ raises $i_C$, which increases the drop across $R_C$ and lowers $v_{CE}$. For a sinusoid the output is shifted by $180^\circ$.

The factor $-g_m R_C$ is the intrinsic gain from $v_{be}$ to $v_{ce}$. With $R_B \gg r_\pi$, most of the input is dropped across $R_B$ and $A_v \approx -\beta R_C / R_B$, so the gain, like the bias, depends on $\beta$.

The gain applies only in the forward-active region. In cutoff or saturation the readout shows no small-signal gain, and an input large enough to reach either limit is clipped in $v_{CE}$.
