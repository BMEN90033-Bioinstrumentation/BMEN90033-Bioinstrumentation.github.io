The cochlear implant as a signal processor

A cochlear implant bypasses the missing or damaged hair cells of the inner ear and stimulates the auditory nerve through an electrode array in the scala tympani. The sound processor divides the microphone signal into frequency bands, extracts the slowly varying envelope of each band, and assigns each envelope to the electrode at the cochlear place tuned to that band. This page follows that chain from the acoustic input to electrode stimulation, and reconstructs an acoustic simulation of the result.

01tonotopy and the electrode array

Place coding along the cochlea

The basilar membrane is tuned along its length. The base, next to the oval window, responds to high frequencies and the apex to low frequencies. A cochlear implant uses this tonotopic map by delivering each frequency band to an electrode at the matching place.

Greenwood's function gives the characteristic frequency at fractional distance $x$ from the apex of the human cochlea:

$$f(x) = A\left(10^{a x} - k\right), \qquad A = 165.4\,\text{Hz},\; a = 2.1,\; k = 0.88$$

The simulation places each electrode at the position obtained by inverting this relation for the centre frequency of its channel. In the Cochlear Nucleus numbering used here, E1 is the most basal contact and carries the highest band; the highest-numbered electrode is the most apical.

  • sound processor: worn behind the ear; contains the microphone and performs the analysis in sections 02 to 05;
  • transmitter coil: sends power and stimulation data across the skin by a radio-frequency link, held in place by a magnet;
  • receiver-stimulator: implanted beneath the skin behind the ear; decodes the link and drives current pulses into each contact;
  • electrode array: platinum contacts on a silicone carrier, inserted into the scala tympani. Nucleus arrays carry 22 intracochlear contacts.
The model distributes electrodes over almost the full spiral. A real array is usually inserted about one to one and a half turns, so the most apical contact sits at a place whose Greenwood frequency is higher than the band assigned to it. This frequency-to-place mismatch is one reason implant users need a period of adaptation.

02the acoustic input

The microphone signal

The processor operates on the sampled microphone signal $x[n]$, here at $f_s = 44.1\,\text{kHz}$. Two synthetic inputs are provided, and a recording of up to 5 s replaces them once made.

  • sweep: a logarithmic chirp from 150 Hz to 8 kHz over 3 s. The excitation moves from the apical electrodes to the basal ones.
  • vowel: a harmonic complex with fundamental $f_0 = 125\,\text{Hz}$, harmonics weighted by formant peaks at 730, 1090 and 2440 Hz, and a 2.5 Hz syllabic envelope.
  • recording: microphone input captured while the record button is held.

Rosen (1992) divides the temporal information in speech into three ranges: the envelope (2 to 50 Hz), periodicity (50 to 500 Hz) and temporal fine structure (above about 600 Hz). Envelope-based implant strategies transmit the first two and discard most of the third.

03the filter bank

Band-pass analysis

The first stage divides the input into $N$ contiguous bands, one per electrode. Centre frequencies are spaced logarithmically between $f_\text{min}$ and $f_\text{max}$:

$$f_i = f_\text{min}\left(\frac{f_\text{max}}{f_\text{min}}\right)^{i/(N-1)}, \qquad i = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$$

Adjacent bands meet at the geometric mean $\sqrt{f_i f_{i+1}}$ of their centre frequencies. The outer edges are $0.8 f_\text{min}$ and $\min(1.2 f_\text{max},\, f_s/2)$. Each band is a second-order high-pass section at its lower edge followed by a second-order low-pass section at its upper edge, both Butterworth biquads:

$$H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{a_0 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}}$$ $$\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi f_c}{f_s}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\sin\omega_0}{2Q}, \qquad Q = 0.707$$

Each section is 3 dB down at its corner and falls at 12 dB per octave beyond it, so neighbouring bands overlap. When a band is narrow the two skirts overlap inside it and its peak gain falls below 0 dB, as the response for the 22-channel preset shows.

Raising $N$ narrows each band without steepening the skirts, so channel independence does not grow in proportion. In an implant, current spread between neighbouring contacts imposes a similar limit: speech recognition in implant users typically stops improving beyond about eight channels (Friesen et al., 2001).

04envelope extraction

Rectification and envelope detection

The envelope of each band signal $y_i[n]$ is obtained by full-wave rectification followed by low-pass filtering:

$$r_i[n] = \left|y_i[n]\right|, \qquad e_i[n] = \left(h_\text{LP} * r_i\right)[n]$$

Rectification moves the energy of an oscillating signal to DC and to even harmonics of its frequency:

$$\left|\sin\omega t\right| = \frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos 2k\omega t}{4k^2 - 1}$$

For a band signal whose amplitude varies slowly, the DC term follows that amplitude. The low-pass filter $h_\text{LP}$, a second-order Butterworth biquad at the envelope cutoff $f_\text{env}$, keeps this slow term and rejects the components near $2 f_i$.

All envelopes are then divided by the largest value across all channels, so the loudest band reaches 1 and the level differences between bands are preserved.

The cutoff decides which temporal cues survive. With the vowel input, harmonics of the 125 Hz fundamental beat within each band, and the voice periodicity appears as ripple on the envelopes at $f_\text{env} = 200\,\text{Hz}$. At 50 Hz the second-order filter attenuates the 125 Hz ripple by about 16 dB, leaving mainly the syllabic envelope.

05stimulation and the vocoder

Carriers, compression and the acoustic simulation

An implant delivers each envelope as the amplitude of a train of biphasic current pulses on its electrode. The channel vocoder is an acoustic model of this process: each pulse train is replaced by a carrier confined to the band, scaled by the envelope, and the channels are summed.

$$v_i[n] = c_i[n]\; g\!\left(e_i[n]\right), \qquad v[n] = \sum_{i} v_i[n]$$
  • sine: $c_i[n] = \sin(2\pi f_i n / f_s)$, a tone at the centre frequency;
  • noise: white noise passed through the band filter and scaled to unit RMS;
  • fine structure: $c_i[n] = \operatorname{sgn}(y_i[n])$, a unit-amplitude carrier that keeps the zero-crossing times of the band signal.

The electrical dynamic range between threshold and maximum comfortable level is much narrower than the acoustic range of speech, typically 10 to 20 dB. The envelope is therefore compressed before it sets the stimulation level. The simulation uses the $\mu$-law,

$$g(e) = \frac{\ln(1 + \mu e)}{\ln(1 + \mu)}, \qquad \mu = 255,$$

which raises low envelope values strongly and leaves the maximum at 1. With compression off, $g(e) = e$.

In the continuous interleaved sampling (CIS) strategy, pulses on different electrodes are interleaved in time so that no two contacts are driven at once, which limits interaction between their electric fields. The Nucleus ACE strategy also selects, in each frame, the subset of channels with the largest envelopes. The simulation stimulates every channel and does not model the pulses.

The fine-structure carrier restores the timing information that envelope strategies discard. Fine-structure strategies such as MED-EL FS4 deliver this timing on the apical, low-frequency channels.
