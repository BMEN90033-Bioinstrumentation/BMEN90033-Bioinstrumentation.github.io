BMEN90033 · WEEK 3 · ATOMIC STRUCTURE

Electron orbitals

The behaviour of semiconductors begins with the electronic structure of the atom. Each electron occupies an orbital, a solution of the Schrödinger equation labelled by the quantum numbers $n$, $l$ and $m$. This page samples the hydrogenic orbitals of hydrogen, carbon, silicon and gold, develops their radial and angular structure, and estimates the energies that order the shells.

01the probability cloud

Hydrogenic orbitals and the probability density

For a one-electron atom the time-independent Schrödinger equation separates in spherical coordinates into a radial and an angular factor:

$$\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\phi) = R_{nl}(r)\, Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)$$

The probability of finding the electron in a volume element $dV$ is $|\psi|^2\,dV$. The three quantum numbers take the values

  • $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, the principal quantum number, which sets the shell;
  • $l = 0, \dots, n-1$, the angular momentum quantum number, written s, p, d, f for $l = 0, 1, 2, 3$;
  • $m = -l, \dots, l$, here the index of the real spherical harmonic, which gives the $p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$ and the five d orientations.

Each point in the cloud is a random sample drawn from $r^2|\psi|^2$, so the density of points follows the probability density. Multi-electron atoms are approximated with hydrogenic orbitals in which the nuclear charge $Z$ is replaced by an effective charge $Z_\text{eff}$; the orbital size then scales as $n^2 a_0 / Z_\text{eff}$, where $a_0 = 52.9\,\text{pm}$ is the Bohr radius.

By the Pauli exclusion principle each orbital holds at most two electrons, of opposite spin. A singly occupied orbital is drawn with half the number of points. Log brightness dims the concentrated inner orbitals so that the outer shells remain visible.

02radial structure

The radial wavefunction and its nodes

The radial factor is a polynomial in $r$ multiplied by a decaying exponential:

$$R_{nl}(r) = N_{nl}\, \rho^{\,l}\, e^{-\rho/2}\, L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(\rho), \qquad \rho = \frac{2 Z_\text{eff}\, r}{n a_0}$$

where $L$ is an associated Laguerre polynomial and $N_{nl}$ normalises the function. The probability of finding the electron in a thin shell between $r$ and $r + dr$ is

$$P(r)\,dr = r^2 R_{nl}^2(r)\,dr$$

The factor $r^2$ is the growth of shell volume with radius, so $P(r)$ vanishes at the nucleus even for s orbitals, whose $R_{nl}$ is largest there.

  • $R_{nl}$ crosses zero $n - l - 1$ times. These radial nodes are spheres on which the electron is never found.
  • The angular factor contributes a further $l$ nodal surfaces, giving $n - 1$ nodes in total.
  • For 1s the most probable radius is exactly $a_0 / Z_\text{eff}$.
The focus orbital sets the radial plot, the cross-section in section 03 and the readouts. The "isolate focus" button in section 01 shows that orbital alone in the 3D scene.

03angular structure

Orbital shape, nodal planes and the sign of $\psi$

The angular factor sets the shape of the orbital. The real spherical harmonics used here are combinations of $e^{\pm im\phi}$ that are real-valued, for example

$$p_x \propto \frac{x}{r},\qquad p_y \propto \frac{y}{r},\qquad p_z \propto \frac{z}{r},\qquad d_{z^2} \propto \frac{3z^2 - r^2}{r^2}$$

Each $p$ orbital therefore has one nodal plane through the nucleus, and $\psi$ takes opposite signs in its two lobes. The probability density $|\psi|^2$ does not show the sign, but the sign decides how orbitals on neighbouring atoms combine: overlapping lobes of the same sign add and form a bonding orbital. This is the basis of the covalent bonds in the silicon crystal.

The plot shows $\psi$ on a plane through the nucleus. A $p_y$ orbital is zero everywhere on the $xz$ plane, which is itself its nodal plane, so the $xy$ plane is needed to see it.

Real orbitals carry no probability current: $\mathbf{j} = (\hbar/m_e)\,\mathrm{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi) = 0$ when $\psi$ is real. A circulating current exists only for a complex state $\psi \propto e^{im\phi}$ with $m \neq 0$.

04shells and screening

Effective nuclear charge and orbital energies

In hydrogen the energy depends only on $n$. In a multi-electron atom the inner electrons screen part of the nuclear charge from the outer ones, and the model replaces $Z$ by an effective charge:

$$E_n \approx -13.6\,\text{eV}\;\frac{Z_\text{eff}^2}{n^2}, \qquad Z_\text{eff} = Z - \sigma$$

where $\sigma$ is the screening by the other electrons. For silicon, with $Z = 14$, a 1s electron sees $Z_\text{eff} = 13.6$, almost the full nuclear charge, while a 3p electron sees only $4.29$. The core electrons are therefore bound far more tightly than the valence electrons in the outermost shell.

  • Silicon has four valence electrons, $3s^2 3p^2$. These take part in the four covalent bonds of the crystal; the core electrons do not.
  • Excitation promotes one electron to an empty higher orbital. The energy must be supplied, for example by an absorbed photon, and is released again on de-excitation.
  • In gold the 5d subshell is full, so the 6s electron is promoted to 6p.
The formula is a rough guide. It reproduces the large spread between core and valence binding energies but overestimates the binding of outer electrons: for the silicon 3p electron it gives about $28\,\text{eV}$, whereas the measured first ionisation energy of silicon is $8.15\,\text{eV}$.
