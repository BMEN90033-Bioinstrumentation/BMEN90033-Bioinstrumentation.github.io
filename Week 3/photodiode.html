How a photodiode detects light

A photodiode is a p-n junction optimised for the detection of light. A photon absorbed in the depletion region creates an electron-hole pair, and the junction field separates the two carriers to produce a photocurrent proportional to the incident photon flux. This page relates reverse bias, photon absorption and the semiconductor bandgap to that photocurrent.

01the unbiased junction

The depletion region as a collecting volume

The p-type region contains mobile holes and fixed acceptor ions; the n-type region contains mobile electrons and fixed donor ions.

Diffusion and recombination of carriers across the junction leave a depletion region of uncompensated ions with few mobile carriers. The ions set up a built-in electric field directed from n to p, and at equilibrium the Fermi level $E_F$ is flat across the device.

For detection the important property of this field is its direction. An electron generated in the depletion region is driven towards the n side and a hole towards the p side, so the two members of a photogenerated pair are separated before they can recombine. The band bending across the junction equals the built-in potential:

$$qV_{bi} = E_{C,p} - E_{C,n}$$
Collection already occurs at zero bias. A photodiode operated without external bias, the photovoltaic mode, relies on the built-in field alone.

02reverse bias

Widening the depletion region

A photodiode is normally operated under reverse bias, with the p side (the anode) negative. The applied voltage $V_R$ adds to the built-in potential, strengthens the field and widens the depletion region:

$$W \propto \sqrt{V_{bi} + V_R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad W = W_0\sqrt{1 + \frac{V_R}{V_{bi}}}$$

The model assumes non-degenerate doping, with each Fermi level $0.1\,\text{eV}$ from its majority band edge, so $qV_{bi} = E_g - 0.2\,\text{eV}$: $0.92\,\text{V}$ for silicon, $0.55\,\text{V}$ for InGaAs and $2.06\,\text{V}$ for GaP. The particle view draws the depletion region up to $4.4\,W_0$. A wider depletion region has two consequences:

  • a larger volume in which photogenerated carriers are collected by drift;
  • a smaller junction capacitance, $C_j = \varepsilon_s A / W$, which shortens the $RC$ time constant of the detector and its load.

In the band diagram, drawn to scale in eV, the n-side bands lie $q(V_{bi} + V_R)$ below the p side, and the quasi-Fermi levels separate by $qV_R$, with $E_{Fn}$ below $E_{Fp}$. Majority electrons on the n side cannot cross the raised barrier.

In this photoconductive mode the only current in the dark is the small reverse leakage of the junction, so the photocurrent stands on a low background.

03photon absorption

Carrier generation and photocurrent

A photon with energy $h\nu \ge E_g$ can be absorbed by lifting an electron from the valence band into the conduction band, which creates an electron-hole pair.

Inside the depletion region the field sweeps the electron to the n side and the hole to the p side. Each absorbed photon therefore transfers one electron charge around the external circuit, and the photocurrent is proportional to the photon flux $\Phi$:

$$I_{ph} = q\,\eta\,\Phi = q\,\eta\,\frac{P}{h\nu} = \mathcal{R}\,P$$
  • $\eta$: quantum efficiency, the fraction of incident photons that yield a collected pair ($0.8$ in the model);
  • $P$: incident optical power, $0$ to $100\,\mu\text{W}$ for the intensity slider;
  • $\mathcal{R}$: responsivity in A/W.

A stronger reverse bias sweeps the carriers out faster, which the particle view shows as a higher separation speed.

The model absorbs photons only within the depletion region. In a real device, pairs generated in the neutral regions within about one diffusion length of the depletion edge also contribute, more slowly, by diffusion.

04spectral response

The bandgap sets the cutoff wavelength

Only photons with $h\nu \ge E_g$ are absorbed. Longer wavelengths pass through the material without generating carriers, which defines a cutoff wavelength:

$$\lambda_c = \frac{hc}{E_g} \approx \frac{1240}{E_g\,[\text{eV}]}\,\text{nm}$$
  • GaP: ultraviolet to green, $\lambda_c \approx 550\,\text{nm}$, $E_g = 2.26\,\text{eV}$;
  • silicon: visible and near infrared, $\lambda_c \approx 1100\,\text{nm}$, $E_g = 1.12\,\text{eV}$;
  • InGaAs: near infrared including the telecommunications band, $\lambda_c \approx 1700\,\text{nm}$, $E_g = 0.75\,\text{eV}$.

Below the cutoff, the responsivity of an ideal detector rises in proportion to wavelength, because a fixed optical power carries more photons when each photon has less energy:

$$\mathcal{R} = \frac{\eta\, q\, \lambda}{hc} \approx \frac{\eta\,\lambda\,[\text{nm}]}{1240}\;\text{A/W}$$

Real responsivity curves fall away at short wavelengths as well, because strongly absorbed light generates its carriers near the surface, where many recombine before collection. The ideal model omits this roll-off.

Silicon photodiodes cover both the red and the near-infrared wavelengths used in pulse oximetry, so a single detector can receive both channels in turn.
