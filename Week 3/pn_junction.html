The pn junction

Joining p-type and n-type silicon creates a region depleted of mobile carriers and a built-in potential barrier across it. An applied voltage raises or lowers that barrier, so the junction conducts readily in one direction and very little in the other. This asymmetry is the basis of the diode and of every bipolar device.

01forming the junction

Diffusion, recombination and the depletion region

At the moment the two materials are joined, the n-side holds many free electrons and the p-side many holes. Each carrier diffuses down its concentration gradient across the junction.

  • electrons crossing into the p-side, and holes crossing into the n-side, recombine with the majority carriers there;
  • the region on either side of the junction is left without mobile carriers, exposing the fixed ionised dopants: $\text{B}^-$ on the p-side and $\text{P}^+$ on the n-side;
  • this space charge sets up an electric field directed from the n-side to the p-side, which drives a drift current opposite to the diffusion current.

Equilibrium is reached when, for each carrier type, drift and diffusion currents cancel. The carrier-free layer is the depletion region, and the potential difference across it is the built-in potential $V_0$.

02band diagram

Band bending and the potential barrier

In equilibrium the Fermi level $E_F$ is constant through the whole structure. Because it lies near $E_C$ on the n-side and near $E_V$ on the p-side, the bands must bend across the depletion region by $qV_0$.

The built-in potential follows from the doping on each side:

$$V_0 = \frac{kT}{q}\,\ln\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}$$

The page takes silicon at $300\,\text{K}$, with $kT/q = 25.85\,\text{mV}$ and $n_i = 1.0\times10^{10}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, and computes $V_0$ from the doping sliders. For $N_A = N_D = 10^{16}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ it is $0.71\,\text{V}$. The Fermi level on each side sits $kT\ln(N/n_i)$ from the intrinsic level, which is taken at midgap.

An applied bias $V$ shifts the barrier to $q(V_0 - V)$. Under bias the junction is no longer in equilibrium, and the single Fermi level splits into quasi-Fermi levels for electrons and holes, separated by

$$E_{Fn} - E_{Fp} = qV.$$
  • forward bias ($V > 0$): the barrier falls, and the number of electrons able to surmount it rises exponentially;
  • reverse bias ($V < 0$): the barrier rises, and only minority carriers generated near the junction are swept across it.
Both axes are to scale: position in micrometres, and energy with the bending, the quasi-Fermi splitting and the $1.12\,\text{eV}$ gap on one scale. The band edges are parabolic on each side of the junction, as Poisson's equation requires for uniform doping, and most of the bending falls on the more lightly doped side. As $V$ approaches $V_0$ the depletion approximation fails, so the diagram shows flat bands for $V \ge V_0$.

03depletion width

Width of the depletion region under bias

Under the depletion approximation, Poisson's equation for an abrupt junction gives the depletion width

$$W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_D}\right)(V_0 - V)}$$

where $\varepsilon_s = 11.7\,\varepsilon_0 = 1.04\times10^{-12}\,\text{F/cm}$ is the permittivity of silicon. For $N_A = N_D = 10^{16}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ the equilibrium width is $0.43\,\mu\text{m}$. Charge neutrality, $N_A x_p = N_D x_n$, divides it between the two sides:

$$x_p = W\,\frac{N_D}{N_A + N_D},\qquad x_n = W\,\frac{N_A}{N_A + N_D}$$

so the region extends furthest into the more lightly doped side. Reverse bias widens it as $\sqrt{V_0 - V}$; forward bias narrows it, and $W$ falls continuously to zero as $V \to V_0$, where the depletion approximation ceases to hold. The region holds equal and opposite fixed charge on its two sides, so it behaves as a capacitor whose plate separation is $W$:

$$C_j = \frac{\varepsilon_s A}{W} \propto (V_0 - V)^{-1/2}$$
A reverse-biased junction is therefore a voltage-controlled capacitor. Varactor diodes use this dependence to tune resonant circuits.

04current-voltage characteristic

The ideal diode equation

The current across the junction is the difference between carriers surmounting the barrier, which rises as $e^{V/V_T}$, and the constant flow of minority carriers swept across it. The result is the Shockley diode equation:

$$I = I_S\left(e^{V/V_T} - 1\right),\qquad V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \approx 25.9\,\text{mV}$$

The model uses $I_S = 1\,\text{pA}$. In forward bias the current rises tenfold for every $V_T \ln 10 \approx 60\,\text{mV}$, and becomes appreciable near $0.5$ to $0.7\,\text{V}$ for silicon. In reverse bias the exponential vanishes and $I \to -I_S$, a current set by thermal generation of minority carriers.

At a large enough reverse voltage the field in the depletion region accelerates carriers until they free further electron-hole pairs by impact ionisation. The multiplication produces a large reverse current: avalanche breakdown. In heavily doped junctions breakdown at a few volts occurs instead by tunnelling (Zener breakdown). The page adds the breakdown term of the SPICE diode model,

$$I = I_S\left(e^{V/V_T} - 1\right) - I_{BV}\,e^{-(V + V_{BR})/V_T},$$

with $V_{BR} = 1.5\,\text{V}$ and $I_{BV} = 1\,\text{mA}$, the reverse current at $V = -V_{BR}$. The characteristic is continuous, and beyond $-V_{BR}$ the reverse current rises tenfold for every further $60\,\text{mV}$. The breakdown term exceeds $I_S$ for reverse voltages beyond about $0.96\,\text{V}$, so the flat $-I_S$ plateau of the log plot ends there.

$V_{BR} = 1.5\,\text{V}$ is a display value chosen to lie within the plotted range; it is not computed from the doping. An abrupt silicon junction doped at $10^{16}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ on its lighter side breaks down near $60\,\text{V}$, and practical diodes range from a few volts to more than $1000\,\text{V}$.
