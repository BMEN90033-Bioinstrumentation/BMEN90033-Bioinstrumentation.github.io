The laser diode

A laser diode is a forward-biased p-n junction in which stimulated emission, rather than spontaneous emission, dominates. Heavy carrier injection produces population inversion in a thin active region, and two cleaved crystal facets form an optical cavity that feeds the emitted light back through the gain medium. This page follows a GaAs device from the unbiased junction to coherent output above threshold.

01the unbiased junction

The p-n junction at equilibrium

With no applied voltage the Fermi levels of the p-type and n-type regions align, and a built-in potential $V_0$ forms across the depletion region.

The fixed acceptor and donor ions in the depletion region set up a field directed from n to p. In the band diagram the conduction and valence bands on the n side lie lower in electron energy than on the p side, by $qV_0$. Electrons fill the bottom of the conduction band on the n side and holes the top of the valence band on the p side, and the barrier prevents either from crossing.

The device modelled here is GaAs, a direct-bandgap semiconductor:

  • $E_g = 1.42\,\text{eV}$, giving emission near $\lambda = 870\,\text{nm}$;
  • $V_0 \approx 1.2\,\text{V}$ built-in potential;
  • refractive index $n \approx 3.5$.
The structure is the same as an LED. The difference lies in the carrier density reached in the active region and in the optical feedback provided by the cavity, developed in the following sections.

02injection and inversion

Carrier injection and population inversion

A forward voltage close to $E_g/q$ lowers the barrier. Electrons from the n side and holes from the p side are injected into the active region, and the current follows the diode law:

$$J = J_0\left(e^{qV/kT} - 1\right)$$

The active region is usually a quantum well, a layer about 5 to 10 nm thick. Confining the injected carriers to this layer raises their density for a given current.

Under injection the electron and hole populations are described by separate quasi-Fermi levels, $E_{Fc}$ and $E_{Fv}$. Their separation grows with current. Net optical gain at photon energy $h\nu$ requires the Bernard-Duraffourg condition:

$$E_{Fc} - E_{Fv} > h\nu \ge E_g$$

The states near the conduction-band edge are then more likely to be occupied than those near the valence-band edge: a population inversion. The current at which $E_{Fc} - E_{Fv}$ reaches $E_g$ is the transparency current. In the model it is $0.8\,I_{th} = 32\,\text{mA}$, and above threshold the separation is clamped.

Below transparency, recombination in the active region is spontaneous and photons leave in random directions; the device behaves as an LED. The particle view shows each recombination as a ring and the emitted photon.

03stimulated emission

Stimulated emission and optical gain

A photon of energy $h\nu \approx E_g$ that passes through the inverted active region can stimulate an electron to recombine with a hole, producing a second photon.

The stimulated photon has the same frequency, phase, polarisation and direction as the incident one. When inversion holds, stimulated emission outweighs absorption and the medium has a positive gain coefficient $g$. The photon flux $\phi$ then grows along the direction of travel:

$$\phi(z) = \phi(0)\, e^{(\Gamma g - \alpha_i)\, z}$$
  • $g$: material gain, set by the joint density of states, the occupation of the bands and the transition matrix element;
  • $\Gamma$: confinement factor, the fraction of the optical mode that overlaps the active region;
  • $\alpha_i$: internal loss from absorption and scattering.
In the particle view, below $I_{th} = 40\,\text{mA}$ photons leave each recombination in random directions. Above it, emission is directed along the axis of the active region, the direction in which the waveguide confines the light.

04optical cavity

The Fabry-Pérot resonator

The cleaved end facets of the crystal act as partial mirrors. At normal incidence the reflectance of a facet between the semiconductor and air is

$$R = \left(\frac{n - 1}{n + 1}\right)^2 \approx 0.3 \quad (n \approx 3.5)$$

The two facets form a Fabry-Pérot resonator of length $L$, typically about $300\,\mu\text{m}$, and photons pass repeatedly through the gain medium. The rear facet is commonly given a high-reflection coating so that most of the light leaves through the front facet. The model uses $R_1 = 0.95$ (rear) and $R_2 = 0.32$ (front).

Lasing begins when the round-trip gain equals the round-trip loss, internal plus mirror:

$$\Gamma g_{th} = \alpha_i + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{R_1 R_2}\right)$$

Only wavelengths for which a whole number of half-wavelengths fits the cavity are reinforced:

$$2nL = m\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta\lambda \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{2nL}$$
For $L = 300\,\mu\text{m}$, $n = 3.5$ and $\lambda = 870\,\text{nm}$ the longitudinal mode spacing is about $0.36\,\text{nm}$, so several modes usually fall within the gain bandwidth of a simple Fabry-Pérot laser.

05coherent output

Threshold and the L-I characteristic

Below the threshold current $I_{th}$ the output is weak spontaneous emission. At threshold the gain reaches the loss, stimulated emission clamps the carrier density, and every further increment of current is converted into stimulated photons.

$$P = \eta_d\,(I - I_{th}), \qquad I > I_{th}$$

The slope efficiency is

$$\eta_d = \eta_i\,\frac{h\nu}{q}\,\frac{\alpha_m}{\alpha_m + \alpha_i}, \qquad \alpha_m = \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{R_1 R_2}\right)$$

where $\eta_i$ is the internal quantum efficiency and $\alpha_m$ the mirror loss. The lateral waveguide sets the transverse mode of the beam, and the cavity modes set its spectral width.

The sharp knee at $I_{th}$ distinguishes a laser from an LED, whose output rises gradually from zero current. The model normalises the output to its value at $100\,\text{mA}$; the beam appears in the particle view only above threshold.
