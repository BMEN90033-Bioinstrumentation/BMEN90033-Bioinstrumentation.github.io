Energy bands in solids

When atoms bond into a crystal, their discrete electron energy levels broaden into bands. The width of the forbidden gap between the highest filled band and the next empty band determines whether a solid is an insulator, a semiconductor or a conductor, and how its conductivity changes with temperature.

Discrete atomic levels broaden into bands

An isolated atom has discrete electron energy levels. When $N$ atoms are brought close enough to bond, the Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons in the crystal from occupying the same state, so each atomic level splits into $N$ closely spaced levels. For a crystal of order $10^{22}$ atoms per cubic centimetre these levels form an effectively continuous band.

Two bands determine the electrical behaviour of the solid:

  • valence band: the highest band that is fully occupied at $0\,\text{K}$. Its electrons form the covalent bonds and cannot carry net current while the band is full;
  • conduction band: the next band above it, empty at $0\,\text{K}$. An electron promoted into it has empty states nearby and moves freely through the crystal;
  • inner shells: lower bands that remain tightly bound to each nucleus and take no part in conduction.

The scene represents energy by radius. Each atom carries a valence shell at a fixed radius and a conduction shell outside it; the radial separation stands for the band gap. As the temperature rises, electrons are promoted from the valence shell and hop between atoms at the radius of the conduction shell.

The shells are a schematic. Electrons in a crystal occupy states that extend over the whole lattice rather than orbits about one atom; only the radial ordering of the shells, which encodes energy, is meaningful.

02the band gap

Insulators, semiconductors and conductors

The band gap is the energy interval between the top of the valence band $E_V$ and the bottom of the conduction band $E_C$, in which the crystal has no allowed electron states:

$$E_g = E_C - E_V$$

An electron can conduct only once it has gained at least $E_g$. At room temperature the thermal energy is $kT \approx 25.9\,\text{meV}$, so the size of the gap relative to $kT$ separates three classes of solid:

  • insulator (SiO$_2$, $E_g \approx 9\,\text{eV}$): the gap is several hundred times $kT$, practically no electrons are promoted, and no current flows;
  • semiconductor (Si, $E_g = 1.17\,\text{eV}$ at $0\,\text{K}$ and $1.12\,\text{eV}$ at $300\,\text{K}$): a small fraction of electrons is promoted, and the number rises steeply with temperature;
  • conductor (Cu): the outer 4s band is only half occupied, so empty states lie directly above the occupied ones and no gap has to be crossed.

Every electron promoted across the gap leaves an empty state, a hole, in the valence band. Neighbouring valence electrons can move into the hole, so the hole also carries current and behaves as a positive charge.

In the diagram the band edges follow the shell radii of the 3D model. The insulator gap is therefore drawn to the same schematic scale as the silicon gap and not in proportion to $9\,\text{eV}$.

Temperature dependence of the gap and the carrier density

The silicon band gap narrows slightly with temperature as the lattice expands and vibrates. The empirical Varshni form describes it:

$$E_g(T) = E_g(0) - \frac{\alpha T^2}{T + \beta}$$

with $E_g(0) = 1.17\,\text{eV}$, $\alpha = 4.73\times10^{-4}\,\text{eV/K}$ and $\beta = 636\,\text{K}$ for silicon.

Between $0$ and $1000\,\text{K}$ the gap falls from $1.17$ to about $0.88\,\text{eV}$; it never closes. In a pure (intrinsic) crystal every promoted electron leaves one hole, so $n = p = n_i$, with

$$n_i = \sqrt{N_C N_V}\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right),\qquad N_C, N_V \propto T^{3/2}$$

where $N_C$ and $N_V$ are the effective densities of states in the conduction and valence bands. The plot normalises $n_i$ to $1.0\times10^{10}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ at $300\,\text{K}$. Silicon has about $5\times10^{22}$ atoms and $2\times10^{23}$ valence electrons per cubic centimetre, so at room temperature fewer than one valence electron in $10^{13}$ is promoted.

The conductivity of the crystal is

$$\sigma = q\,(n\,\mu_n + p\,\mu_p) = q\,n_i\,(\mu_n + \mu_p)$$

and rises with temperature because $n_i$ rises. In a metal the carrier density is fixed and lattice scattering lowers the mobility, so its conductivity falls with temperature.

The exponential term dominates. From $300$ to $400\,\text{K}$ it multiplies $n_i$ by about 230, while the $T^{3/2}$ factor and the narrowing of the gap each contribute a factor of about 1.5.
