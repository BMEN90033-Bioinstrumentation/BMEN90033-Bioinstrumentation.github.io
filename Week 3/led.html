How an LED emits light

A light-emitting diode is a forward-biased p-n junction made from a direct-bandgap semiconductor. Forward bias injects electrons and holes across the junction, where they recombine and release the bandgap energy as photons. This page follows that process from the unbiased junction to the dependence of emission wavelength on the semiconductor material.

01the unbiased junction

Diffusion, the depletion region and the built-in field

The p-type region contains mobile holes and fixed, negatively charged acceptor ions. The n-type region contains mobile electrons and fixed, positively charged donor ions.

When the two regions are joined, holes diffuse into the n side and electrons into the p side, and carriers near the junction recombine. The region either side of the junction is left with few mobile carriers and an uncompensated space charge of ionised dopants: the depletion region. The space charge sets up a built-in electric field directed from n to p, which drives a drift current that opposes further diffusion.

At equilibrium drift and diffusion balance, the Fermi level $E_F$ is constant across the device, and the bands bend by the built-in potential $V_0$. For non-degenerate doping it is

$$V_0 = \frac{kT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}\right)$$
  • $N_A$, $N_D$: acceptor and donor concentrations;
  • $n_i$: intrinsic carrier concentration of the semiconductor;
  • $kT/q$: thermal voltage, about $25.9\,\text{mV}$ at $300\,\text{K}$.

In terms of the band edges, $qV_0 = E_g - (E_C - E_F)_n - (E_F - E_V)_p$. LED junctions are heavily doped; the model places each Fermi level $0.05\,\text{eV}$ inside its majority band, so $qV_0 = E_g + 0.1\,\text{eV}$: $2.74\,\text{V}$ for InGaN, $2.05\,\text{V}$ for AlGaInP and $1.52\,\text{V}$ for GaAs. The band diagram is drawn to this scale.

The barrier $qV_0$ prevents electrons in the n-type conduction band from entering the p side. In the particle view the electrons are reflected at the edge of the depletion region.

02forward bias

Lowering the barrier to carrier injection

A source connected with its positive terminal to the p side (the anode) opposes the built-in potential. The barrier falls to $q(V_0 - V)$ and the depletion region narrows:

$$W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s\,(V_0 - V)}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_D}\right)}$$

The number of carriers able to surmount the barrier rises exponentially with the applied voltage, which gives the diode equation

$$I = I_S\left(e^{V/nV_T} - 1\right)$$

with saturation current $I_S$, ideality factor $n$ and thermal voltage $V_T = kT/q$. Because of the exponential, the current is negligible until $V$ approaches a turn-on voltage and then rises steeply. For an LED the turn-on voltage is close to $E_g/q$, so wider-gap materials need a higher forward voltage.

Under bias the system is no longer in equilibrium. The single Fermi level separates into quasi-Fermi levels $E_{Fn}$ for electrons and $E_{Fp}$ for holes, separated by the junction voltage: $E_{Fn} - E_{Fp} = qV_j$. The band offset across the junction falls to $q(V_0 - V_j)$.

The model treats the turn-on voltage $V_{th}$ of each material as sharp. Below it the whole applied voltage appears across the junction, $V_j = V$, and electrons remain on the n side. Above it the current is limited by the series resistance of the neutral regions, which takes the excess voltage, so $V_j$ stays at $V_{th}$ and electrons are injected across the junction. The depletion width follows $W = W_0\sqrt{(V_0 - V_j)/V_0}$.

03recombination

Radiative recombination and photon emission

Electrons injected into the p side are minority carriers among a high density of holes. An electron that drops from the conduction band into an empty state in the valence band recombines with a hole and releases an energy close to the bandgap $E_g$.

In a direct-bandgap semiconductor such as GaAs, GaN or InGaN, the conduction-band minimum and the valence-band maximum occur at the same crystal momentum, so the transition can emit a photon without any other particle taking part. The photon energy and wavelength are related by

$$E_\text{ph} = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \approx E_g$$

In the particle view each injected electron emits one photon when it first falls into a vacancy. It then continues through the valence band by moving into neighbouring vacancies, so the vacancies themselves move towards the junction. This is the hole current that supplies further recombination.

In an indirect-bandgap semiconductor such as silicon, the transition also requires a phonon to conserve momentum. Radiative recombination is then improbable and most of the energy is released as heat, which is why silicon is not used for LEDs.

04colour and material

The bandgap sets the emission wavelength

Since the photon energy is close to $E_g$, the peak emission wavelength follows from the bandgap alone:

$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{E_g} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda\,[\text{nm}] \approx \frac{1240}{E_g\,[\text{eV}]}$$

Compound semiconductors are alloyed to place the bandgap where it is needed:

  • InGaN: blue to green, $\lambda \approx 450$ to $530\,\text{nm}$, $E_g \approx 2.4$ to $2.8\,\text{eV}$;
  • AlGaInP: red to amber, $\lambda \approx 590$ to $650\,\text{nm}$, $E_g \approx 1.8$ to $2.1\,\text{eV}$;
  • GaAs: near infrared, $\lambda \approx 870\,\text{nm}$, $E_g \approx 1.4\,\text{eV}$.

Changing the alloy composition, for example the indium fraction in InGaN, shifts the bandgap and therefore the colour. The emission line is not monochromatic: thermal spread of the carriers broadens it to a width of tens of nanometres.

Pulse oximeters use this dependence directly. A red LED near $660\,\text{nm}$ and an infrared LED near $940\,\text{nm}$ illuminate the tissue at two wavelengths at which oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin absorb differently.
