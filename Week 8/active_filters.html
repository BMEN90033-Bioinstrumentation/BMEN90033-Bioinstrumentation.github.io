BMEN90033 · WEEK 8 · ACTIVE FILTER FAMILIES

Active filter families and pole-zero placement

A buffered cascade of first-order stages can only place poles on the negative real axis. Active topologies allow each pole pair to be placed anywhere in the left half plane and zeros to be introduced on the imaginary axis. The four classical lowpass families differ only in where they place these singularities. This page derives each placement from its geometric origin: the pole circle of the Butterworth response, the pole ellipse of the Chebyshev Type I, the imaginary-axis zeros of the Chebyshev Type II, and the combined pole-zero pattern of the elliptic response.

01from cascade to family

Pole placement defines a filter family

A cascade of $N$ identical buffered first-order stages has $N$ repeated real poles at $s = -1/RC$. The magnitude response is

$$|H(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{(1 + (\omega/\omega_c)^2)^{N/2}},$$

which has slope $-20N\,\text{dB/decade}$ at high frequency but a soft transition near $\omega_c$. The response only reaches its asymptotic slope well into the stopband. The passband edge is gradual because every pole sits on the same point on the negative real axis: no resonance sustains the magnitude near the edge.

Once an op-amp synthesises complex pole pairs, each pair has two degrees of freedom: the distance from the origin sets the natural frequency, and the angle from the negative real axis sets the damping. A pole close to the imaginary axis produces a nearly resonant response that lifts the magnitude near that frequency. The allocation of these degrees of freedom across the $N$ poles, together with the decision to add zeros on the imaginary axis, defines a filter family.

The four classical lowpass families

The prototypes are shown on a normalised frequency scale. Butterworth, Chebyshev Type I, and elliptic responses use $\omega_p = 1\,\text{rad/s}$ as the passband edge; Chebyshev Type II is referenced to a stopband edge $\omega_s = 1\,\text{rad/s}$. In each case, the magnitude is at most unity. They differ in the optimisation criterion:

  • Butterworth. The passband is maximally flat. There are no zeros, and the poles lie on a circle.
  • Chebyshev Type I. The passband is equiripple and the stopband is monotone. There are no zeros, and the poles lie on an ellipse.
  • Chebyshev Type II. The passband is monotone and the stopband is equiripple. The zeros lie on the imaginary axis, and the poles are the reciprocals of those of a Chebyshev Type I prototype.
  • Elliptic (Cauer). Both bands are equiripple. The zeros lie on the imaginary axis, and the poles lie near, but not on, a Chebyshev Type I ellipse.
The accompanying plot shows the magnitude response of each family at $N = 4$. The marker at $\omega = 1$ is the passband edge for Butterworth, Chebyshev Type I and elliptic, and the stopband edge for Chebyshev Type II. A tighter constraint on the magnitude shape restricts the poles to more specific locations. The Butterworth response is the least constrained and the elliptic response the most.

02butterworth

Butterworth: poles on a circle

The Butterworth response is defined by a single condition on the magnitude:

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + (\omega/\omega_c)^{2N}}.$$

This makes the first $2N - 1$ derivatives of $|H(j\omega)|^2$ vanish at $\omega = 0$. The passband is as flat as a rational function of degree $N$ allows, hence the name "maximally flat".

Origin of the poles

The defining magnitude condition implies, on the imaginary axis,

$$H(s)\,H(-s) = \frac{1}{1 + (s/j\omega_c)^{2N}}.$$

The $2N$ roots of $1 + (s/j\omega_c)^{2N} = 0$ are evenly spaced on a circle of radius $\omega_c$ centred at the origin. Exactly $N$ roots lie in the left half plane and $N$ lie in the right half plane. A causal stable filter takes the $N$ left-half-plane roots as the poles of $H(s)$:

$$s_k = \omega_c \, \exp\!\left(j \, \frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{2k - 1 + N}{N}\right), \quad k = 1, 2, \ldots, N.$$

Geometric origin of the circle

The denominator $1 + (s/j\omega_c)^{2N}$ depends on $s$ only through $s^{2N}$, so its roots satisfy $(s/j\omega_c)^{2N} = -1$. Every root therefore has $|s| = \omega_c$, and adjacent roots are separated by an angle $\pi/N$. The circle of radius $\omega_c$ follows directly from the magnitude condition depending on $\omega$ only through the single power $\omega^{2N}$.

A pole closer to the imaginary axis produces a sharper resonance at its imaginary frequency. With every Butterworth pole on the same circle, some pole lies near $j\omega$ for every $\omega \le \omega_c$, but none lies close enough to dominate. The passband is therefore flat and free of ripple.

03chebyshev type i

Chebyshev Type I: poles on an ellipse

The Chebyshev Type I response exchanges the maximally flat passband for a sharper transition. The magnitude obeys

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon^2 \, T_N^2(\omega/\omega_c)},$$

where $T_N$ is the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind. Across the passband, $T_N$ remains bounded between $-1$ and $+1$, so $|H(j\omega)|^2$ oscillates between $1$ and $1/(1 + \varepsilon^2)$. The ripple amplitude is set by $\varepsilon$.

From ripple to ellipse

Solving $H(s)H(-s) = $ constant for the poles on the locus where $T_N$ oscillates gives a closed form. Let $\mu = (1/N)\,\sinh^{-1}(1/\varepsilon)$. The $N$ left-half-plane poles are

$$\small \begin{aligned} s_k &= -\sinh\mu \, \sin\theta_k \;+\; j \cosh\mu \, \cos\theta_k, \\ \theta_k &= (2k - 1)\,\frac{\pi}{2N}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, N. \end{aligned}$$

These coordinates are the parametric equation of an ellipse with semi-minor axis $\sinh\mu$ along the real axis and semi-major axis $\cosh\mu$ along the imaginary axis. As the ripple shrinks, $\varepsilon \to 0$ sends $\mu \to \infty$ and $\sinh\mu / \cosh\mu \to 1$, so the ellipse becomes asymptotically circular after the usual frequency renormalisation.

Geometric origin of the ellipse

The same Chebyshev polynomials that minimise the maximum deviation on $[-1, 1]$ over polynomials of degree $N$ have evenly spaced extrema in the angle variable. Mapping those extrema onto the imaginary axis and lifting them off through a real-axis offset $\sinh\mu$ gives the ellipse. The horizontal compression by the factor $\sinh\mu / \cosh\mu < 1$ moves every pole closer to the imaginary axis than its Butterworth counterpart, which sharpens each pole's resonance and accelerates the rolloff. The cost appears in the passband: the closer poles produce multiple peaks where the response touches unity, separated by dips of $1/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon^2}$.

A larger ripple corresponds to a smaller $\mu$, a narrower ellipse, and poles closer to the imaginary axis. For the same order, a Chebyshev Type I rolls off faster than a Butterworth, and the transition steepens as the tolerated passband ripple increases. The aspect ratio of the ellipse sets the passband ripple, and the Butterworth response is the zero-ripple limit after renormalisation.

04chebyshev type ii

Chebyshev Type II: zeros on the imaginary axis

The Type II response (also called inverse Chebyshev) reverses the Type I compromise. The passband is monotone, and the equiripple behaviour is moved into the stopband:

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^2 \, T_N^2(\omega_s/\omega)}}.$$

Here $\omega_s$ is the stopband edge. The argument $\omega_s/\omega$ inverts the frequency variable, so the Chebyshev oscillation that was in the passband for Type I is mapped into the stopband.

Origin of the zeros

Whenever $T_N(\omega_s/\omega) = 0$, the reciprocal term diverges and the magnitude goes to zero. The zeros occur at $\omega_s/\omega = \pm \cos\theta_k$, that is

$$\omega_{z,k} = \frac{\omega_s}{\cos\theta_k}, \qquad \theta_k = (2k - 1)\,\frac{\pi}{2N}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, \lfloor N/2 \rfloor.$$

These zeros sit on the imaginary axis above the stopband edge. Each zero produces a notch in the response: since $|j\omega - j\omega_{z,k}| = 0$ exactly at $\omega = \omega_{z,k}$, the magnitude is forced to zero there. The notches alternate with local maxima because, between adjacent zeros, the ratio of zero distances to pole distances determines the stopband ripple envelope.

Origin of the poles

The poles of a Chebyshev Type II filter are the reciprocals of a Chebyshev Type I prototype with the same $N$ and ripple parameter:

$$s_{k,\,\text{II}} = \frac{1}{s_{k,\,\text{I}}^*}\bigg|_{\text{LHP}}.$$

Geometrically, each Type I pole is inverted in the unit circle: its angle from the negative real axis, and hence its $Q$, is preserved, while its distance from the origin changes from $|s|$ to $1/|s|$. Type I poles with $|s| > 1$ move inward and those with $|s| < 1$ move outward. The Type II pole pattern is the inversion of the Type I ellipse, which is why this filter is also called the inverse Chebyshev.

Geometric origin of the placement

Zeros on the imaginary axis are the only means by which a rational $H(s)$ can produce stopband ripple at finite frequencies. The pole reciprocation is dictated by the requirement that the passband be monotone: the product of pole distances along the passband must vary smoothly, with no peaks. Inversion of the Chebyshev I ellipse satisfies this requirement. The result is a flat passband, a sharper transition than Butterworth (because the imaginary-axis zeros reduce the magnitude), and equiripple stopband attenuation set by $\varepsilon$ (smaller $\varepsilon$ means a lower stopband ripple envelope between the notches).

A Chebyshev Type II response is appropriate when the stopband must meet a specified attenuation with controlled equiripple peaks between transmission zeros. The magnitude does not decrease to zero monotonically: it falls sharply, rises, falls again and, for even $N$, approaches a non-zero stopband floor between the highest zero and infinity.

05elliptic (cauer)

Elliptic: ripple in both bands

The elliptic, or Cauer, response is the magnitude that achieves the steepest possible transition for a given order, ripple, and stopband attenuation. It generalises the Chebyshev family by allowing equiripple behaviour in both bands at once:

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon^2 \, R_N^2(\omega, \xi)},$$

where $R_N(\omega, \xi)$ is the elliptic rational function with selectivity parameter $\xi = \omega_s/\omega_p$. $R_N$ is bounded between $\pm 1$ on the passband and has an equiripple envelope in the stopband, with poles that become transmission zeros of $H(s)$. This makes both bands equiripple after the $1/(1 + \varepsilon^2 R_N^2)$ transformation.

Pole and zero placement

Two sets of singularities appear:

  • Zeros on the imaginary axis at the poles of the elliptic rational function $R_N$. These produce notches in the stopband, as in the Chebyshev Type II response.
  • Poles in the left half plane, near but not on the Chebyshev Type I ellipse for the same ripple, with no simple closed-form locus. The pole pair nearest the passband edge lies just inside the ellipse and closer to the imaginary axis, which sharpens the corner; lower-frequency poles lie outside it.

The closed form involves Jacobi elliptic functions:

$$\omega_{z,r} = \frac{1}{\kappa \, \mathrm{cd}\!\left(\dfrac{(2r - 1) K(\kappa^2)}{N},\, \kappa^2\right)}, \qquad r = 1, \ldots, \lfloor N/2 \rfloor,$$

where $\mathrm{cd} = \mathrm{cn}/\mathrm{dn}$ is a Jacobi elliptic function and $K$ the complete elliptic integral, both evaluated at modulus $\kappa = 1/\xi$. The pole locations come from a related elliptic transcendental equation involving a second modulus tied to $\varepsilon$. The plots use $0.5\,\text{dB}$ passband ripple and $\xi = 1.6$; with these fixed, the stopband attenuation is set by the order: $9.6$, $24.1$, $39.2$ and $54.3\,\text{dB}$ for $N = 2$ to $5$.

Geometric origin of the placement

An elliptic filter allocates every degree of freedom to the transition. Each pole-zero pair locally steepens the transition: the zero on the imaginary axis at $\omega_{z,r}$ reduces the magnitude sharply, and the matched pole just inside the passband edge sustains the passband edge against that zero. The pairs are placed so that the local minima in the passband are equal and the local maxima in the stopband are equal, by the equioscillation theorem. No other rational placement of the same number of poles and zeros achieves a steeper transition for the same ripple and floor.

Setting the stopband attenuation to infinity reduces the elliptic response to a Chebyshev Type I; setting the passband ripple to zero reduces it to a Chebyshev Type II; setting both to their respective limits recovers the Butterworth. The elliptic response is the general case; the other three families are special cases obtained by removing the passband ripple, the stopband ripple, or both.

06side by side

The four families together

Plotted at the same order on a normalised frequency scale, the four families show the consequence of each compromise:

  • Butterworth has the smoothest passband but the slowest transition.
  • Chebyshev Type I exchanges passband flatness for transition steepness.
  • Chebyshev Type II keeps the passband flat and gains transition steepness through stopband notches.
  • Elliptic accepts ripple in both bands and has the steepest transition for the same order.

The s-plane plot overlays the pole and zero patterns. The Butterworth poles lie on a circle, the Chebyshev I poles on a horizontally compressed ellipse, the Chebyshev II poles on the reciprocal ellipse with zeros on the imaginary axis, and the elliptic poles near a Chebyshev I ellipse with zeros also on the imaginary axis. Each step toward a sharper transition moves the low-frequency poles closer to the imaginary axis and, for the Type II and elliptic responses, adds zeros that produce notches in the stopband.

The choice of family determines where ripple is accepted. Biopotential amplifiers use Bessel filters when flat group delay is the priority and Butterworth filters when a flat magnitude passband is the priority; antialiasing stages favour elliptic or Chebyshev II for sharp transitions; pulse-detection front ends sometimes favour Chebyshev I for its fast rolloff with the simplest topology. The s-plane geometry represents this compromise directly.
