Real-time measurement of the mean absolute value of an EMG signal

Design brief

A wearable EMG device must measure the mean absolute value of the signal in real time. The input is assumed to be a sinewave.

01the input

Input signal and target value

The brief specifies a sinewave input, $x(t) = A\sin(\omega t)$. The mean absolute value is the time average of $|x(t)|$ over a window $T$. For a sinewave of amplitude $A$ and a window that contains a whole number of half-cycles, the integral has a closed form and the MAV is a constant.

Continuous
$ \mathrm{MAV} = \tfrac{1}{T}\!\int_{0}^{T} |x(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t $
Discrete (sampled)
$ \mathrm{MAV} = \tfrac{1}{N}\!\sum_{n=1}^{N} |x[n]| $

For $x(t) = A\sin(\omega t)$, evaluating the integral gives $\mathrm{MAV} = \tfrac{2A}{\pi} \approx 0.637\,A$. The target value is plotted as a dashed line, against which the chain output in later slides can be compared.

The equation requires two operations: the absolute value of $x(t)$, followed by the mean of that absolute value. The following slides construct each operation from circuit blocks covered in the course.

02two operations

Decomposition of the equation into two stages

The MAV equation is the composition of two operators: an absolute value of the instantaneous signal, and an integral over a finite window. Each maps to a standard analogue block.

Step 1 · $|\cdot|$
Absolute value
$\,y_1(t) = |x(t)|$. Each negative half-cycle is reflected about zero. The hardware implementation is a full-wave rectifier.
Step 2 · $\langle\cdot\rangle$
Running mean
$\,y_2(t) = \tfrac{1}{T}\!\int_{t-T}^{t} y_1(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau$. The DC component of $y_1$ is the MAV; the AC ripple at $2\omega$ is rejected by a low-pass filter.

A rectified sinewave is the sum of a DC term equal to $\tfrac{2A}{\pi}$ and a Fourier series of even harmonics of $\omega$. Averaging, or low-pass filtering with a cutoff well below $2\omega$, isolates that DC term.

03stage 1 · full-wave rectifier

Absolute value with a precision rectifier

A passive bridge of four diodes rectifies a sinewave, but each conducting diode subtracts $V_D \approx 0.6$ V from the signal. EMG amplitudes are of the order of millivolts, so the diodes do not conduct. A precision rectifier places the diode inside an op-amp feedback loop, which divides the diode drop by the open-loop gain, so the output follows $|x(t)|$ down to nearly zero.

For a sinewave input $x(t) = A\sin(\omega t)$, the rectified output is

$|x(t)| \;=\; A\bigl|\sin(\omega t)\bigr| \;=\; \dfrac{2A}{\pi} \;-\; \dfrac{4A}{\pi}\!\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\!\dfrac{\cos(2k\omega t)}{4k^{2}-1}$

The first term is the DC value that the next stage must extract. The remaining terms are harmonics of $2\omega$, the lowest of which is at $2\omega$ itself. A low-pass filter with a cutoff well below $2\omega$ removes them.

Strengths
  • Negative half-cycles are reflected exactly, with no diode-drop offset.
  • Signals as small as a few millivolts are rectified, which a passive bridge cannot achieve.
  • The output is a continuous function, so the next stage averages without gating.
Weaknesses
  • A dual-supply op-amp with adequate slew rate at the EMG bandwidth is required.
  • The output contains harmonics of $2\omega$ that must be filtered before display.

04stage 2 · averaging

Mean extraction with a low-pass filter

Averaging in continuous time is integration over a window. A first-order RC low-pass filter is the analogue approximation: the output is a weighted moving average of the input with an exponential window of time constant $\tau = RC$ and cutoff $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)$.

The rectified signal is a DC term at $\tfrac{2A}{\pi}$ plus harmonics at $2\omega, 4\omega, \dots$. A first-order low-pass filter with cutoff $f_c$ has magnitude $|H(f)| = 1/\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}$. Choosing $f_c \ll 2f_\mathrm{sig}$ attenuates every harmonic; the DC term passes unchanged, so the filter output settles to $\tfrac{2A}{\pi}$, which is the MAV.

Strengths
  • A single resistor and capacitor occupy negligible area on a wearable PCB.
  • The cutoff $f_c$ sets the trade-off between ripple and settling time through $\tau = RC$.
  • The DC gain is unity, so the output gives the MAV in volts without a calibration constant.
Weaknesses
  • A lower $f_c$ gives a slower response, so the device cannot follow sudden changes in the envelope.
  • The first-order roll-off is $-20$ dB/decade, so residual $2\omega$ ripple remains unless further stages are cascaded.
Lower cutoff frequencies suppress ripple but increase settling time. The dashed line marks the analytic target $\tfrac{2A}{\pi}$, so any deviation visible at steady state is residual ripple rather than a bias.

05the full chain

Block diagram of the MAV signal chain

The two stages connect in series. The EMG sinewave enters the precision rectifier, the rectified signal feeds a first-order low-pass filter, and the filter output is the running mean absolute value. The chain is entirely analogue and runs continuously, so the output is available in real time without sampling or accumulation in software.

  • The preamplifier (an instrumentation amplifier) raises the millivolt EMG to a level well above the input-referred noise of the rectifier op-amp.
  • The precision rectifier outputs $|x(t)|$ with no diode-drop offset.
  • The low-pass filter with cutoff $f_c \ll 2f_\mathrm{sig}$ extracts the DC component, which equals $\tfrac{2A}{\pi}$ and is the MAV.

06application · myoelectric prosthesis

MAV control of a prosthetic gripper

A myoelectric prosthetic hand is controlled by surface EMG from the residual limb. The raw signal is bipolar and noisy, so the controller cannot use it directly. The MAV envelope correlates with voluntary contraction force, and the controller therefore computes this signal chain in real time.

  • Detection: a threshold on the MAV separates a voluntary contraction from baseline activity, so the gripper engages only when a grip is intended.
  • Proportional control: above the threshold, the MAV value sets the motor drive, so a stronger contraction produces a firmer grip.
  • Averaging method: three options for the averaging stage are provided. The first-order LPF is the analogue default; the moving-window average implements $\tfrac{1}{T}\!\int|x|\,\mathrm{d}t$ directly; a 1-D Kalman filter estimates $\log\sigma^2$ from $\log x^2$ (after Sanger, 2007).
  • Latency and smoothness: the smoothing parameter $f_c$ sets the bandwidth of all three. A lower $f_c$ rejects more EMG burst noise but lengthens the delay between intent and grip. $f_c = 0$ is the limit of pure averaging.
Drag the effort slider to simulate a contraction. Switching between the three smoothers and lowering $f_c$ towards zero flattens the trace. The Kalman option uses fixed process and measurement variances, so its gain converges to a constant, set here to match the first-order LPF at the same $f_c$. It then acts as a first-order smoother on $\log\sigma^2$ rather than on $|x|$. An adaptive gain would require time-varying process or measurement variances.
